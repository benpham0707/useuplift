The summer I turned sixteen, my parents announced we were moving from Atlanta to a small town in Oregon I had never heard of. Bend, population forty thousand, surrounded by mountains I had only seen on postcards. I cried for three days.

My first week at Summit High School felt like arriving on another planet. In Atlanta, my friend group had been mostly Black. Here, I could count the Black students on one hand. People stared. Not meanly, exactly, but with a curiosity that made me feel like a museum exhibit. A girl in my English class asked if she could touch my hair. I said no, then went to the bathroom and sat in a stall until the bell rang.

I threw myself into cross-country running because it required zero social skills. Just me, the trail, and the sound of my own breathing against the pine-scented air. Coach Davis noticed I was fast and asked me to join varsity. I said yes mostly because practices meant less time sitting alone in the cafeteria.

Running changed things slowly. My teammate Eliza started waiting for me after practice so we could stretch together. She told me about her anxiety, and I told her about missing Atlanta. We didn't have much in common on paper, but loneliness is a universal language.

By winter, I had a small group. We weren't the popular kids, but we showed up for each other. When I organized a Juneteenth celebration at school, my friends helped me plan it even though most of them had never heard of the holiday. Twenty-seven students came. Not a huge number, but twenty-seven more than zero.

I still miss Atlanta sometimes. I miss the noise and the culture and feeling like I belong without trying. But Bend taught me something Atlanta never could: that belonging isn't something you find. It's something you build, one awkward conversation at a time.

I used to think moving was the worst thing that ever happened to me. Now I think it was just the hardest. There is a difference.
