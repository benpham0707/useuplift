The first time I dissected a frog, I cried. Not because I was squeamish but because I had named him. Gerald. I was twelve and I had just read a book about amphibian population collapse and it seemed cosmically wrong to be cutting open something the planet was running out of. My biology teacher, Mrs. Okoro, handed me a tissue and said, "Good. You should feel something. Scientists who don't feel anything are dangerous."

I have thought about that sentence at least once a week for five years.

I think about it when I am ankle-deep in the marsh behind the county water treatment plant, cataloging the species we find in our monthly biodiversity survey. Four springs ago, we counted six amphibian species in that marsh. Last April: four. The pickerel frogs vanished first, then the gray treefrogs. Nobody at the water treatment plant noticed because nobody was counting. We were counting.

I think about it during the twenty-three-minute bus ride to the community college where I take organic chemistry on Tuesday and Thursday evenings, sitting between a nursing student named Dawn who quizzes me on nomenclature and a retired electrician named Phil who is taking the class, he says, "because I never got to the first time." The three of us share a textbook because Dawn cannot afford her own and Phil says sharing builds character. I suspect Phil cannot afford his own either.

I think about it in my bedroom at two in the morning, staring at the chromatography results from the water samples I collected downstream of the old textile mill on Route 9. The dissolved oxygen levels are lower than they should be, and the phosphate concentrations are higher, and I do not have the equipment to determine whether the correlation means what I think it means. But I have a hypothesis, and a county environmental officer who agreed to review my data, and a stubbornness that my mother describes as "a gift wrapped in a problem."

Mrs. Okoro was right. I should feel something. But feeling is not enough.

The marsh is shrinking. The frogs are disappearing. The water downstream of the mill is wrong in ways I can almost but not quite prove. And I am seventeen years old with a secondhand copy of Principles of Environmental Science and a bus pass.

Here is what I have learned about the distance between caring and doing: it is not bridged by passion. Everyone is passionate. It is bridged by tedium. By showing up at the marsh on cold mornings when nobody else wants to. By learning organic chemistry not because it is fun but because I need it. By sitting with data that refuses to tell me what I want to hear and asking better questions instead of easier ones.

I named the frog Gerald because I wanted him to matter. That was naive, maybe. But the impulse underneath it, the refusal to look at a living thing and see only a specimen, that is the impulse I want to build a career on.

Gerald mattered. Not because I named him, but because his absence means something. Science is the practice of noticing what is missing and refusing to look away.

I do not look away.
