My mother keeps a laminated list of English phrases in her apron pocket. "How much does this cost." "My son is in school." "I am sorry, I do not understand." She has carried this list for eleven years, since we came to Houston from Guatemala, and the creases have worn the lamination soft as cloth.

I found the list when I was fourteen, doing laundry. I unfolded it and read each phrase in her careful handwriting, the Spanish translations penciled underneath in case she forgot. And I understood, for the first time, the weight of what she navigated every day while I complained about algebra homework.

My mother cleans three houses a week. She leaves at five in the morning and comes home smelling of bleach and other people's lavender detergent. She has never once asked me to feel sorry for her. What she asks is this: "Did you do your homework? Did you eat? Are you being good?" Three questions, every night, in that order.

I started interpreting for her when I was nine. Parent-teacher conferences, lease agreements, phone calls with the electric company. The hardest was the time she needed to explain a billing error at the clinic and the receptionist kept talking faster, as if volume could substitute for patience. I was eleven. I didn't know the word for "deductible." I made one up. It worked.

By high school, I had become the family's unofficial lawyer, accountant, and customer service representative. I learned to code-switch between the Spanish of home and the English of institutions, between being a kid and being the adult in the room. My friends worried about prom dates. I worried about whether our landlord's repair promise was legally binding.

I do not tell this story to inspire pity. I tell it because my mother's laminated list taught me that language is not just communication. It is power. The people who control the language control the conversation, and the people shut out of it are at the mercy of those who bother to translate.

I want to study linguistics not because I love grammar diagrams, though I do, but because I have seen what happens when language fails. I have sat in waiting rooms where a woman's pain was dismissed because she could not describe it in the right words. I want to build bridges between those words.

My mother still carries the list. She does not need it as much anymore, she has learned more English than she gives herself credit for, but she keeps it like a talisman. I asked her once why she does not throw it away.

"Because it reminds me of the day I decided to stay," she said.

I carry my own list now. Not laminated, not in my pocket. But I keep it: the words I have borrowed, the silences I have filled, the questions I still need to learn how to ask.
