Every Saturday morning for the past three years, I have volunteered at the Riverside Community Garden on the east side of town, the part that doesn't have a Whole Foods or a farmer's market. Our twelve raised beds feed about forty families through the summer growing season.

I started volunteering because my school required community service hours. I will be honest about that. But I kept coming back because of Mrs. Okafor, a Nigerian grandmother who tends the tomato beds like they are her grandchildren. She taught me that you can tell if soil is healthy by smelling it. Good soil smells like rain. Dead soil smells like nothing.

My role evolved from pulling weeds to managing our seed library and teaching newer volunteers basic composting techniques. Last spring, I wrote a small grant proposal to the city parks department and we received twelve hundred dollars for new irrigation supplies. It was my first time writing anything that wasn't for a grade, and seeing the money come through felt different from getting an A on a paper.

The garden is not changing the world. Twelve raised beds cannot solve food insecurity. But when Mr. Hernandez brings his daughters to pick green beans, or when Mrs. Chen trades her bok choy for Mrs. Okafor's peppers, something happens that I do not think you can get at a grocery store. People who would never otherwise meet each other start sharing recipes and childcare tips and complaints about the weather.

I am still figuring out what community means. But I think it might have something to do with dirt under your fingernails and knowing your neighbor's name.
