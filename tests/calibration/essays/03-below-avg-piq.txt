Growing up as a first-generation Vietnamese American, I often felt caught between two worlds. At home, my parents spoke Vietnamese and we ate pho for dinner most nights. At school, I spoke English and tried to fit in with my friends. It was confusing to navigate these two different cultures.

One experience that really shaped me was when my grandmother came to live with us from Vietnam. She didn't speak any English and I became her translator. I had to go to doctor's appointments with her, help her at the grocery store, and explain American customs. It was a lot of responsibility for a fourteen-year-old.

Sometimes I felt embarrassed about my family's differences. When my friends came over, I worried they would think our house smelled weird from my mom's cooking. But over time, I started to appreciate my heritage more. I realized that being bilingual was actually a strength, not a weakness.

Now I see my dual identity as something that makes me unique. I can connect with people from different backgrounds because I understand what it feels like to be an outsider. I want to study sociology in college so I can better understand how culture shapes identity. My experience as a first-generation American has given me a perspective that I think will be valuable in the classroom and beyond.

I am proud of who I am and where I come from, and I want to use my experiences to help other immigrant families navigate the challenges of living between two cultures.
