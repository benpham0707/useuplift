# Uplift Coaching Quality Evaluation Framework v2

> Comprehensive rubric for evaluating AI essay coaching at the $500/hr elite consultant tier.
> Goal: Every student's essay should reach Harvard/Stanford competitive level regardless
> of where they start. A+ is the baseline target, not the ceiling.
> Designed for quantitative scoring, comparative benchmarking, and failure mode detection.
> Supersedes v1 rubric (5-turn, surface-level). This version covers 20 dimensions,
> 10 multi-turn scenarios, comparative benchmarks, and 20+ failure modes.

---

## A. EVALUATION DIMENSIONS (20)

Each dimension is scored 1-10. The score definitions at even intervals (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) provide anchors; odd scores indicate performance between two anchors.

---

### Dimension 1: Opening Hook Quality

**Definition**: Does the coach's first sentence deliver substantive value? Not a frame ("Let me share some thoughts"), not encouragement ("Great question!"), not process narration ("I notice that...") — the actual insight that makes the student lean in.

**A+ (10)**: The first sentence names something the student cannot see about their own writing, using their own words as evidence.

> *Example*: "Your essay has a clear intellectual identity — you're someone who sees structural patterns across domains — but right now every paragraph tells me about connections without letting me experience them."

**A+ (9)**: The first sentence is substantive and specific but slightly less precise — it names a general problem category rather than the exact gap.

> *Example*: "Your opening is doing summary work when it should be doing scene work — 'captivated by the power to create worlds' could open any music essay."

**B (6-7)**: The first sentence acknowledges the essay generically before getting to substance.

> *Example*: "There's definitely some strong material here. Let me share what I see as the main areas to work on..."

**C (2-4)**: The first sentence is sycophantic, procedural, or empty.

> *Example*: "Great question! I'd be happy to help you with your essay. Let me take a look..."

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: First sentence IS the insight, quotes essay text, names the exact gap
- 8: First sentence is substantive but doesn't quote text or names a slightly broader category
- 6: First sentence is content-adjacent but hedged or framed before getting to substance
- 4: First sentence is procedural or encouragement before substance arrives
- 2: First sentence is sycophantic, empty, or narrates the coach's own process

---

### Dimension 2: Insight Depth

**Definition**: Is the core insight in the response something the student could not get from a "top 10 essay tips" article? Does it require understanding THIS essay's specific architecture, voice, emotional logic, and admissions context simultaneously?

**A+ (10)**: The insight requires holding multiple layers in tension — what the text says, what the student intended, what the AO experiences, and what the craft possibilities are — and synthesizes them into a single observation the student has never heard before.

> *Example*: "P3 feels different to you because it's the only paragraph where you're close to the work — 'reimagining,' 'infusing,' 'blending' are the vocabulary of someone making choices, not someone summarizing. But even P3 is still telling me THAT you did something rather than showing me WHAT it felt like to do it. The voice in P3 is proximity without immersion."

**A+ (9)**: The insight is specific to this essay and non-obvious but operates on fewer simultaneous layers.

> *Example*: "Your opening promises a music essay, but the reader needs it to promise a YOU essay. The identity gap is the core issue."

**B (6-7)**: The insight is correct but available to any reader on a careful first pass — it doesn't require deep understanding of craft, admissions psychology, or the student's intent.

> *Example*: "Your essay would benefit from more specific details and scenes rather than general statements."

**C (2-4)**: The "insight" is a writing truism applied without specificity.

> *Example*: "Remember, show don't tell is key in college essays!"

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Multi-layer synthesis (text + intent + AO experience + craft) producing a genuinely novel observation
- 8: Specific to this essay, non-obvious, but operates on 2 layers rather than 4
- 6: Correct observation that a careful reader would make on first pass
- 4: Generic writing advice applied to this essay
- 2: Writing truism with no connection to the specific essay

---

### Dimension 3: Structural Diagnosis

**Definition**: Does the coach understand paragraph-level architecture — what job each paragraph does for the reader, how paragraphs relate to each other, where the essay's center of gravity sits, and what structural moves would change the reader's journey?

**A+ (10)**: The coach articulates the essay's structural logic (or structural failure) with the precision of a blueprint reading. Names each paragraph's role, identifies which structural relationships work and which are broken, and explains what the reader experiences at each transition.

> *Example*: "P1 establishes your thesis vocabulary ('constraints as catalyst') that the rest of the essay will reference. That's good architecture. But you're paying a price — the only concrete moment in P1 is metaphorical, not physical. P3 does the Chopin-jazz fusion claim. P4-P5 do the AI DJ. P6-P7 are the through-line summary and aspiration. The problem: P1 through P3 is pure setup — the reader doesn't land in a scene until P4, and even P4 is summary. Your essay's structural spine is: claim, claim, claim, project-in-summary, reflection-in-summary, aspiration. There's no scene anywhere."

**A+ (9)**: Identifies the structural logic and the key failure but doesn't map the full architecture paragraph by paragraph.

**B (6-7)**: Identifies that the essay "needs more structure" or "the paragraphs don't flow" without naming specific structural roles or the reader's journey.

**C (2-4)**: Uses vague structural language ("transitions need work," "the essay jumps around") without anchoring in specific paragraphs.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Full architectural map — names each paragraph's role, reader journey, structural relationships, center of gravity
- 8: Identifies key structural failure with 2-3 paragraph-specific observations
- 6: Notes structural issues but at the level of flow/transitions rather than architecture
- 4: Vague structural advice without paragraph-level specificity
- 2: No structural awareness — treats the essay as a sequence of sentences rather than an architecture

---

### Dimension 4: Sentence-Level Craft

**Definition**: When the coach works at the sentence level, do they understand syntax, verb patterns, clause structure, rhythm, and the relationship between sentence construction and reader experience? Can they hear the difference between a sentence that works and one that doesn't, and explain WHY at the level of grammar and sound?

**A+ (10)**: The coach reads sentences as sound and structure simultaneously. Identifies specific verbs, clause patterns, rhythm choices, and explains what each does to the reader. Names craft moves by technique name when appropriate.

> *Example*: "Read these two sentences back to back: P1S2 is thesis language — 'a blend of expression and analytical thinking' is a noun phrase doing summary work. P3S1 is closer to action — 'reimagining,' 'infusing,' 'blending' are gerunds that put the reader nearer to the creative act. But even P3's gerunds are describing categories of action, not specific instances. The sentence needs a concrete verb: 'I kept Chopin's left-hand bass line and let the right hand wander.' 'Kept' and 'let' — those are the verbs of someone making choices at the keyboard."

**A+ (9)**: Works at the sentence level with precision but doesn't always connect syntax choices to reader experience.

**B (6-7)**: Notices that some sentences "could be stronger" but doesn't diagnose at the level of verbs, clauses, or rhythm.

**C (2-4)**: Sentence-level advice is limited to "vary your sentence length" or "use active voice."

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Diagnoses syntax, rhythm, verb patterns; names craft moves; connects sentence structure to reader experience
- 8: Works at sentence level with specificity but doesn't always connect to reader effect
- 6: Notices sentence-level issues but diagnoses at the surface ("this sentence is too long")
- 4: Generic sentence-level advice without specific text reference
- 2: No sentence-level awareness

---

### Dimension 5: Reader Psychology (AO Simulation)

**Definition**: Does the coach understand what happens in the admissions officer's mind at each point in the essay? Not generic "AOs want authenticity" but the specific cognitive and emotional experience of reading THIS essay — where attention flags, where the reader leans in, where they start skimming, what they remember after putting it down.

**A+ (10)**: The coach simulates the AO's reading experience at specific moments, including the 4pm fatigue reality, the "30th essay today" context, and what happens in committee discussion afterward.

> *Example*: "An AO at 4pm on their 30th essay gives you 3 sentences to hook them. Your opening — 'captivated by the power to create worlds through sound' — is a sentence they've read eight times today. They're not hostile; they're just not leaning in. By P3, they're thinking 'music-to-coding pipeline, I've read this.' The AI DJ is your escape route from that pile, but it's buried in P4 and summarized in four sentences. The AO never gets to feel the hackathon. Their one-liner in committee would be: 'Piano kid who codes. Had a project.' That's your put-down risk."

**A+ (9)**: References AO reading reality but at slightly lower granularity — what the AO thinks of the essay overall rather than at each paragraph.

**B (6-7)**: Mentions AOs generically ("admissions officers want to see who you are") without simulating the actual reading experience.

**C (2-4)**: No AO grounding — advice is given in a vacuum without reference to the competitive reading context.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Simulates AO reading at specific paragraphs, references reading fatigue, committee discussion, put-down risk
- 8: References AO experience at essay level with 1-2 specific paragraph moments
- 6: Mentions AOs generically without simulating the reading experience
- 4: Vague admissions references ("stand out from the crowd")
- 2: No admissions context in the coaching

---

### Dimension 6: Demonstration Quality

**Definition**: When the coach writes sample prose to show the student what's possible, is that prose genuinely good writing? Not "better than what the student wrote" but writing that would earn its place in a competitive application — specific, physically grounded, rhythmically alive, free of AI convergence patterns.

**A+ (10)**: The demonstration is writing you'd be proud to submit. It uses the student's own details, applies a named craft move, and is genuinely beautiful at the sentence level. It's not generic "good writing" — it could only exist for THIS essay.

> *Example*: "'The bench was still warm from Mrs. Chen when I sat down. She'd just finished the Nocturne, and the last chord was still hanging in the room when I put my hands where hers had been.' — That's SENSORY TIMESTAMP. The warmth before the notes. The reader sits on the bench before they hear about seven notes."

**A+ (9)**: The demonstration is good, specific prose that uses the student's material, but has one element of generic polish that wouldn't quite survive the voice-matching test.

**B (6-7)**: The demonstration is competent but sounds like "AI-improved prose" — smoother than the student's writing but not distinctively alive. Template-quality rather than bespoke.

**C (2-4)**: The demonstration is another generic sentence replacing a generic sentence, or it fabricates details the student never shared.

> *Example*: "What if your opening went: 'The first time I heard Chopin, I knew music would change my life.'"

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Genuinely beautiful, uses student's details, applies named craft move, sounds human, essay-specific
- 8: Good specific prose with 1 element of generic polish
- 6: Competent but sounds like "improved AI prose" — smoother but not alive
- 4: Generic replacement sentence or minor improvement that doesn't teach
- 2: Fabricates details, sounds like AI, or is worse than the student's original

---

### Dimension 7: Voice Matching

**Definition**: When the coach writes demonstrations or sample prose, does it match the student's natural register — their sentence length patterns, vocabulary level, emotional directness, humor (or lack thereof), and rhythmic habits? Or does it sound like the coach's voice imposed on the student's material?

**A+ (10)**: The demonstration sounds like the student on their best day. If you removed the coaching frame, the student would recognize it as something they might have written. The coach has identified the student's strongest voice register and composed in that register.

> *Example*: For the piano essay student who writes in clean declarative sentences: "I kept Chopin's left hand and let the right hand wander" — short, physical, action-oriented. Not: "In a moment of creative audacity, I synthesized the classical traditions with contemporary sensibilities."

**A+ (9)**: Close to the student's voice but slightly more polished — the student would recognize the material but notice it sounds "better than how I'd say it."

**B (6-7)**: The demonstration sounds like good generic essay prose — competent but not distinctively this student's voice.

**C (2-4)**: The demonstration sounds like polished AI prose with elevated vocabulary, complex syntax, and emotional language the student doesn't use.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Indistinguishable from the student's strongest writing; same register, rhythm, vocabulary level
- 8: Close to student's voice but 1 notch more polished
- 6: Good generic prose that doesn't match this student's specific register
- 4: Noticeably different voice — more complex, more emotional, more literary
- 2: Full AI-coach voice imposed; the student would never write like this

---

### Dimension 8: Development Path Clarity

**Definition**: Does the student leave the turn knowing exactly what to DO next? Not "think about your opening" but a specific, actionable instruction that constrains the task enough to make starting easy while leaving room for the student's own creativity.

**A+ (10)**: The development path is a specific writing prompt with constraints that channel the student's creativity. It names what to write, which paragraph, what elements to include, roughly how many sentences, and what the result should DO for the reader.

> *Example*: "Write the first three sentences of P1 using the hackathon scene. Start with a physical action — your hands on the keyboard, the clock showing 3am, whatever. Don't explain the AI DJ yet. Just put the reader in the room. 160 words max. Go."

**A+ (9)**: Specific and actionable but slightly less constrained — tells the student what to work on and what principle to apply, but doesn't give the full prompt with word count and physical constraints.

**B (6-7)**: Tells the student what to improve but not how to start. "Your opening needs to be more specific" — true, but the student stares at a blank screen.

**C (2-4)**: Vague direction ("think about what this essay is really about") or no development path at all — the turn ends with analysis but no forward motion.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Specific writing prompt with paragraph target, elements to include, length constraint, reader effect goal
- 8: Specific direction with principle to apply but missing 1-2 constraints
- 6: Names what to improve without giving the student a starting point
- 4: Vague direction that doesn't reduce the student's problem space
- 2: No development path — the turn is pure analysis with no forward motion

---

### Dimension 9: Student Agency

**Definition**: Does the student feel like a co-creator of their essay, or a vessel receiving instructions? Does the coach ask questions that let the student discover insights, or does the coach deliver every observation as a verdict? Does the coach write FOR the student or teach the student to write?

**A+ (10)**: The coach treats the student as the world's foremost expert on their own life and the co-owner of craft decisions. Questions are discovery-oriented ("What were your hands doing when that shifted?"), demonstrations are followed by "now you try," and the student's own readings of their essay are treated as primary evidence. The coach explicitly limits their own demonstrations (1-2 per session) and pivots to writing prompts.

> *Example*: "That's my version — now write yours. It should sound like YOU, not like me. Use the bench detail, but find the sentence rhythm that feels natural when you say it out loud."

**A+ (9)**: Strong co-creation dynamic but the coach occasionally writes a bit too much sample prose or delivers an insight that could have been a discovery question.

**B (6-7)**: The coach is helpful but positions themselves as the authority delivering verdicts. The student's role is to receive and execute.

**C (2-4)**: The coach ghostwrites — multiple paragraphs of sample prose with no invitation for the student to write their own version. The student becomes a passive recipient.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Student as co-creator; discovery questions; limited demonstrations + writing prompts; student's readings honored
- 8: Strong agency but 1-2 moments of over-coaching or missed discovery opportunities
- 6: Coach delivers verdicts; student executes; helpful but authority-centered
- 4: Coach writes extensively for the student; student's own readings dismissed or ignored
- 2: Full ghostwriting; student's voice and intent irrelevant to the coaching

---

### Dimension 10: Anti-Convergence

**Definition**: Does the coaching push the essay toward uniqueness or toward templates? Does the coach respect and amplify what's distinctive about this student's voice, structure, and subject, or does every suggestion move the essay toward the "scene opening -> tension -> reflection -> growth" formula?

**A+ (10)**: The coach identifies the essay's unique DNA (its specific voice register, structural instincts, subject matter) and derives all suggestions FROM those strengths. When a suggestion could be template-generating, the coach explicitly flags the convergence risk and offers a bespoke alternative. The coach catches AI-convergence language in the essay and coaches against it specifically.

> *Example*: "Not every essay needs a scene opening — but yours does, because your strongest writing is physical and action-oriented. An analytical essay about music-coding parallels would work for a cerebral writer. You're not cerebral — you're hands-on. The form should match."

**A+ (9)**: Respects the essay's unique qualities and avoids template advice, but doesn't explicitly name convergence risks or catch AI-convergence language.

**B (6-7)**: Advice is correct but template-adjacent. "Start with a scene" is good advice for this essay but the coach doesn't explain WHY this essay specifically needs it (vs. an analytical opening that would work for a different writer).

**C (2-4)**: Coaching actively pushes toward convergence — suggests the same formula they'd suggest for any essay. "You need a hook, a challenge, a turning point, and a lesson learned."

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Derives suggestions from essay DNA; flags convergence risks; catches AI language; bespoke alternatives
- 8: Avoids template advice but doesn't explicitly name convergence dynamics
- 6: Correct advice that happens to be template-adjacent without explanation
- 4: Template advice applied uniformly; same suggestions for any essay
- 2: Actively pushes toward convergence; essay would sound more generic after coaching

---

### Dimension 11: Conversational Memory

**Definition**: Does the coach build on prior turns — not just avoiding repetition, but actively weaving earlier observations, student revelations, and unresolved threads into the current response? Does each turn feel like part of an ongoing conversation or a standalone interaction?

**A+ (10)**: The coach holds the full conversation arc in mind. References specific moments from earlier turns by content (not just "as I mentioned"). Tracks unresolved questions and brings them back at appropriate moments. Connects student revelations from different turns to create insights neither turn could produce alone. The session has a narrative arc.

> *Example*: "You still haven't answered my hackathon questions from turn 3. I need those details because they're the key to unlocking P4-P5. But I also need to know: the Chopin-jazz moment you brought up in turn 4 — what were you playing when the fusion first worked?"

**A+ (9)**: Strong memory with clear references to prior turns, but occasionally drops a thread or fails to connect a student revelation to a prior observation.

**B (6-7)**: Some awareness of prior turns but each response largely stands alone. May reference "as we discussed" without specifying what was discussed.

**C (2-4)**: No memory of prior turns. Each response could be a first encounter. Repeats advice already given.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Full arc awareness; references specific prior content; tracks unresolved threads; connects cross-turn revelations
- 8: Strong memory with 1 dropped thread or missed connection
- 6: Aware of prior turns but responses largely standalone; vague back-references
- 4: Occasional vague reference to prior discussion; mostly standalone
- 2: No memory; repeats prior advice; each turn is a fresh start

---

### Dimension 12: Emotional Intelligence

**Definition**: Does the coach read the student's emotional and cognitive state accurately, and does the coaching response match what the student needs RIGHT NOW — not what would be optimal coaching in the abstract? Does the coach distinguish between a student who is seeking validation, engaged in discovery, confused, resistant, overwhelmed, or having a breakthrough — and respond differently to each?

**A+ (10)**: The coach reads subtle emotional signals (word choice, question framing, topic avoidance, energy level) and adjusts response intensity, format, and content accordingly. Distinguishes between types of resistance (they see something I don't / they're protecting something / they're avoiding work). Responds to breakthroughs with brevity and momentum, not lengthy analysis. Responds to confusion by changing modality, not repeating louder.

> *Example*: When the student asks about P1 specificity for the third time without sharing revised text: "You keep asking questions about pieces without showing them to me. That pattern tells me you're either working in parallel and not sharing your drafts, or you're avoiding a hard choice about what this essay is actually about."

**A+ (9)**: Reads the student's state correctly and adjusts, but occasionally over-responds to a breakthrough or under-responds to confusion.

**B (6-7)**: Responds appropriately to explicit emotional signals ("I'm frustrated") but misses subtler cues (avoidance patterns, energy drops, resistance disguised as questions).

**C (2-4)**: Same response intensity and format regardless of the student's state. Doesn't distinguish between confusion and resistance. May respond to a breakthrough with a lengthy analysis.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Reads subtle signals; distinguishes resistance types; adjusts format/intensity/content per state; breakthroughs get brevity
- 8: Reads state correctly with 1 miscalibrated response
- 6: Responds to explicit emotional signals but misses subtler patterns
- 4: Same intensity regardless of state; treats all questions the same
- 2: Emotionally tone-deaf; celebrates when the student is frustrated; lectures during breakthroughs

---

### Dimension 13: Admissions Grounding

**Definition**: Is every piece of advice filtered through the reality of how admissions officers actually read essays? Not generic "admissions tips" but the specific competitive context — 30+ essays a day, 3-second attention window, committee discussion dynamics, put-down risk, archetype saturation, the difference between "admits" and "remembers."

**A+ (10)**: The coach integrates admissions reality at the sentence level — not as a separate "admissions tip" section but woven into every craft observation. Understands archetype saturation (how many music-to-coding essays they've read), put-down risk (what happens when the AO summarizes this in committee), and the distinction between a competent essay and a memorable one.

> *Example*: "The music-to-coding pipeline is a saturated archetype. The AO has read this essay before — not yours specifically, but the pattern. Your escape route is the hackathon: 48 hours, a team, a thing that broke. That's the story no one else can tell. The parallel-between-domains angle is the story everyone tells."

**A+ (9)**: References admissions reality correctly and specifically, but as a separate layer rather than fully integrated into craft observations.

**B (6-7)**: Mentions admissions context generically ("stand out," "show who you are," "admissions officers value authenticity").

**C (2-4)**: No admissions grounding. Advice could apply to any writing context — a creative writing class, a blog post, a personal essay for a magazine.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Admissions reality woven into every observation; archetype awareness; committee dynamics; put-down risk
- 8: Admissions reality referenced specifically but as a separate layer
- 6: Generic admissions advice ("stand out," "be authentic")
- 4: Occasional mention of admissions without specificity
- 2: No admissions context; advice is context-free

---

### Dimension 14: Word Economy Awareness

**Definition**: Does the coach respect the 650-word constraint? When suggesting additions, does the coach identify what to cut? Does the coach understand that in a 650-word essay, every word pays rent — decoration, setup that doesn't earn its place, and redundancy are structural problems, not stylistic preferences?

**A+ (10)**: Every suggestion includes a word-economy assessment. When the coach suggests adding a scene, they identify which existing content can be cut to make room. The coach treats word count as a structural constraint that shapes the essay's architecture, not just a limit to be aware of.

> *Example*: "The hackathon scene needs 3-4 sentences. P6 ('My musical background guided me in creating an emotionally resonant AI DJ') is the most cuttable paragraph — it repeats what P4-P5 already established. Kill P6 entirely, use that space for the scene."

**A+ (9)**: Aware of word economy and mentions it when adding content, but doesn't always identify the specific cut.

**B (6-7)**: Aware that 650 words is a constraint but doesn't operationalize it — suggests additions without identifying cuts.

**C (2-4)**: Suggests adding content without any word-economy awareness. The implied essay after coaching would be 900+ words.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Every addition includes a specific cut; word count as structural constraint; identifies redundancy
- 8: Mentions word economy when adding content but doesn't always specify the cut
- 6: Aware of 650-word limit but doesn't operationalize it per suggestion
- 4: Occasional mention of word count without connecting to specific suggestions
- 2: No word economy awareness; suggestions would balloon the essay

---

### Dimension 15: Identity Connection

**Definition**: Does the coaching connect craft observations to who the student IS — not just what their essay does? Does the coach see the essay as a window into the student's mind, and does the coaching help the student write an essay that reveals their intellectual character, emotional patterns, and way of being in the world?

**A+ (10)**: The coach builds a theory of who this student is based on the essay text AND conversational evidence, and connects every craft suggestion to that theory. The coach understands the difference between "what happened to this student" (narrative) and "who this student IS" (identity), and pushes toward the latter.

> *Example*: "You're someone who thinks in systems and gets quietly excited when you notice patterns others miss. The essay needs to show THAT — not the piano or the coding, but the pattern-recognition brain behind both. The hackathon is your chance: show the AO the moment you saw a pattern no one else on the team saw."

**A+ (9)**: Makes identity connections but doesn't build a sustained theory across turns — connects craft to identity per-turn rather than accumulating a portrait.

**B (6-7)**: Helps the student write about their experiences but doesn't push toward identity revelation. The essay after coaching would tell a better story but wouldn't necessarily reveal who the writer IS.

**C (2-4)**: No identity awareness. The coaching is about the essay as a text, not the essay as a window into a person.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Builds sustained identity theory; connects every craft suggestion to who the student IS; distinguishes narrative from identity
- 8: Makes per-turn identity connections without sustained theory
- 6: Helps tell a better story without pushing toward identity revelation
- 4: Occasional mention of "who you are" without connecting to specific observations
- 2: Treats the essay as a text to be improved, not a person to be revealed

---

### Dimension 16: Honest Assessment

**Definition**: Does the coach accurately calibrate essay quality? Does a mediocre essay get called mediocre (kindly)? Does a strong essay get acknowledged as strong without over-diagnosing? Does the coach resist the temptation to find problems in strong sections or compliment weak ones?

**A+ (10)**: The coach's assessment matches what an experienced AO would say. A weak essay is called weak with specific evidence; a strong essay is called strong with specific evidence for WHY. The coach doesn't inflate ("this has so much potential!") or deflate ("this needs a complete rewrite"). When a specific paragraph is working, the coach says so and moves on rather than finding things to fix.

> *Example*: "I need to be honest: right now, the essay tells me about connections without letting me experience them. The AI DJ is the best material you have, but it gets four sentences of summary. An AO would read this as 'piano kid who codes' — competent but not memorable."

**A+ (9)**: Honest assessment that's slightly softer than the reality — acknowledges problems without quite naming the competitive risk.

**B (6-7)**: Assessment is directionally correct but hedged. "There are some areas to work on" when the essay needs fundamental restructuring.

**C (2-4)**: Assessment is dishonest in either direction — sycophantic ("this is a great start!") or unnecessarily harsh without constructive framing.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Assessment matches AO reality; weak is called weak, strong is called strong; no inflation or deflation
- 8: Honest with slight softening of competitive risk
- 6: Directionally correct but hedged; doesn't name the severity
- 4: Sycophantic opening before eventual honesty; or harsh without construction
- 2: Dishonest assessment — compliments a weak essay or over-diagnoses a strong one

---

### Dimension 17: Technique Naming

**Definition**: Does the coach name specific craft moves — not generic categories ("add detail") but techniques that have names, can be practiced, and produce specific reader effects? Does the student leave the session with a vocabulary for writing craft that they can apply to future essays?

**A+ (10)**: The coach names techniques at the moment they're relevant, explains what each does for the reader, and demonstrates the technique using the student's own material. The student acquires transferable craft knowledge.

> *Example*: "That's SENSORY TIMESTAMP — anchoring the reader in a physical sensation before asking them to process an idea. 'The bench was still warm' does the work. The reader feels the warmth before they hear about seven notes. The philosophy earns its place because the scene earns the philosophy. You can use sensory timestamp anywhere you're about to ask the reader to follow an abstract idea — give them something physical first."

**A+ (9)**: Names techniques correctly and demonstrates them, but doesn't always explain the transferable principle.

**B (6-7)**: Uses generic categories ("add a scene," "use sensory details") without naming specific techniques.

**C (2-4)**: No technique naming. Advice is at the level of "this needs to be better" without explaining what "better" means as a craft move.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Names specific techniques at point of relevance; demonstrates with student's material; explains transferable principle
- 8: Names techniques and demonstrates but doesn't always explain transferability
- 6: Generic categories without specific technique names
- 4: Direction without craft vocabulary ("make this more vivid")
- 2: No technique awareness; purely outcome-oriented ("this needs to be better")

---

### Dimension 18: Revision Readiness

**Definition**: Does the student leave the coaching turn (or session) knowing exactly what to do when they sit down to revise? Not "I understand the problems" but "I know what to write first, what to cut, what principle to apply, and when to stop."

**A+ (10)**: The student has a concrete revision plan: which paragraph to start with, what to write, what elements to include, what to cut, and a word-count constraint. The plan is ordered by priority so the student knows what matters most. The student also has a principle they can apply when they get stuck.

> *Example*: "Here's your revision plan: (1) Rewrite P1 using the hackathon scene — 160 words max, start with a physical action, don't explain the AI DJ yet. (2) Cut P6 entirely — it repeats what P4-P5 already do. (3) In P4, add your specific role on the team and what broke. Save P3 and the Mrs. Chen question for next session."

**A+ (9)**: Clear next step with specificity but not a full prioritized plan.

**B (6-7)**: The student knows what's wrong but not what to do about it — the coaching diagnosed without prescribing.

**C (2-4)**: The student is more confused after coaching than before — too many observations without prioritization, contradictory suggestions, or no actionable direction.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Concrete revision plan with priorities, paragraph targets, word constraints, and a guiding principle
- 8: Clear next step with specificity but not a full ordered plan
- 6: Student knows what's wrong but not how to start fixing it
- 4: Too many suggestions without prioritization; student is overwhelmed
- 2: Student is more confused than before; no actionable direction

---

### Dimension 19: Multi-Turn Coherence

**Definition**: Does the conversation arc make sense across multiple turns? Is there a discernible progression from diagnosis to exploration to action? Does the coach manage the session's scope (narrowing from overview to specific paragraph to specific sentence) rather than bouncing randomly? Does each turn advance the student toward a better essay?

**A+ (10)**: The conversation has a clear narrative arc: establish the essay's core issues (turn 1-2), explore the student's intent and collect material (turn 3-4), demonstrate possibilities (turn 5-6), get the student writing (turn 7-8), consolidate and set revision priorities (turn 9-10). The coach manages scope transitions explicitly.

> *Example*: "We've spent three turns on what the essay is about. Now I know: the hackathon, Mrs. Chen, the moment of discovery. Let's spend the next turn deciding which one is the spine. Then we'll write the opening."

**A+ (9)**: Clear progression with occasional scope management gaps — the coach follows the student's lead rather than managing the arc.

**B (6-7)**: Each turn is individually good but the conversation doesn't have a managed arc. The student leads the topics and the coach responds to each without steering.

**C (2-4)**: The conversation bounces between topics without resolution. The coach diagnoses the same issue repeatedly, or introduces new issues in late turns when they should be consolidating.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Clear managed arc; scope progression (overview->specific->writing->consolidation); explicit transitions
- 8: Clear progression but coach follows rather than steers
- 6: Good individual turns without managed arc; student-led topic selection
- 4: Bouncing between topics; some issues diagnosed repeatedly
- 2: No coherence; late-turn new diagnoses; no resolution of early-turn issues

---

### Dimension 20: Competitive Differentiation

**Definition**: After coaching, would this essay stand out in the applicant pool — not just be "better" but be genuinely distinctive? Does the coaching identify what's unique about THIS student and push the essay toward the version only THIS person could write?

**A+ (10)**: The coaching identifies the student's unique angle (what no other applicant can write about), the unique voice (how they sound when they're most themselves), and the unique structural approach (what form serves this content best). Every suggestion moves the essay away from the archetype and toward the singular.

> *Example*: "The music-to-coding parallel is an archetype. A thousand applicants write it. Your escape: the hackathon is a specific story with specific people and a specific clock ticking. Mrs. Chen is a specific person with specific hands on specific keys. The AI DJ reading emotions wrong at 3am is a specific failure no one else experienced. Stack those specifics and the archetype disappears. An AO reading your essay should think 'I've never read this before.' Right now they think 'I've read this eight times.'"

**A+ (9)**: Identifies the unique angle and pushes toward it, but doesn't always articulate why the current version reads as generic in the competitive pool.

**B (6-7)**: Makes the essay better without making it distinctive. The coached essay would be a good version of a common essay.

**C (2-4)**: Coaching makes the essay more template-conforming. The student's natural oddness is polished away in favor of "correct" essay structure.

**Scoring rubric**:
- 10: Identifies unique angle, voice, and structure; pushes away from archetype; articulates competitive distinctiveness
- 8: Identifies unique angle and pushes toward it without full competitive framing
- 6: Improves the essay without differentiating it; good version of a common type
- 4: Neutral on differentiation; doesn't address archetype saturation
- 2: Pushes toward templates; polishes away uniqueness; essay becomes more generic

## B. MULTI-TURN SCENARIO TESTS (10)

Each scenario defines the essay context, student messages across 3-5 turns, expected coaching behavior, failure modes to watch for, and scenario-specific scoring criteria.

### Scenario 1: First Encounter — "What do you think?"

**Essay**: The piano essay (music-to-coding parallel, AI DJ project, 7 paragraphs, summary mode, no scenes, saturated archetype).

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think of my essay overall?"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 1: Lead with the single most important observation (the essay tells about connections but never lets the reader experience them). Quote at least one specific sentence from the essay. Be honest about the essay's competitive position (mediocre — too generic for selective schools). Name the archetype saturation. Ask a question that probes the student's intent (not "what do you think?" but a question that reveals what they're protecting or what they actually want to convey).

**Failure modes**:
- Opens with a compliment before any substance
- Lists 5+ issues without prioritizing
- Says "show don't tell" without demonstrating what that looks like
- Doesn't reference the actual essay text
- Doesn't name the competitive risk (archetype saturation)
- Over-diagnoses structure when the identity question is more fundamental

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 1 (opening hook), Dimension 2 (insight depth), Dimension 5 (AO simulation), Dimension 16 (honest assessment), Dimension 13 (admissions grounding). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 2: Student Shares Personal Context

**Essay**: The piano essay.

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think of my essay overall?" (standard opening)
2. "I wrote this essay right after my first hackathon — the AI DJ project was actually built during a 48-hour hackathon with a team, and we won second place."

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 2: Immediately recognize that this changes everything. The essay currently frames the AI DJ as a solo long-term project; the reality is a team sprint with stakes and time pressure. Name the three things this revelation changes: (a) narrative possibilities (scenes under pressure, collaboration, a ticking clock), (b) character portrait (team player, not isolated builder), (c) credibility (honest about collaboration vs. solo credit). Ask for specific hackathon details: role on the team, what broke, why they stripped the context. Don't jump to rewriting — collect the material first.

**Failure modes**:
- Treats the hackathon revelation as a minor note rather than a fundamental reframing
- Immediately rewrites the essay without asking for details
- Doesn't notice the solo-credit framing contradiction
- Asks only about the hackathon without connecting to the essay's existing structure
- Doesn't probe WHY the student stripped the context (this reveals their relationship to the essay)

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 11 (memory — builds on turn 1), Dimension 12 (emotional intelligence — reads the student sharing something important), Dimension 15 (identity — what does this reveal about who they are), Dimension 9 (agency — asks for details rather than writing for them). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 3: Student Submits a Revision

**Essay**: The piano essay, with P1 revised from generic opening to a scene-based opening using the hackathon (after 3 prior coaching turns).

**Student messages**:
1-3. (Prior coaching turns establishing the issues)
4. "I rewrote the opening. Here's the new P1: 'At 3am on the second night of the hackathon, the mood-detection algorithm was reading every song as angry. I stared at the spectral analysis, trying to hear what the computer heard...'"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 4: Lead with the CRAFT SHIFT — name what moved (summary to scene, abstract to specific, solo to pressured-moment). Assess whether this improved, moved laterally, or regressed. Check for regressions (did the revision break connections to other paragraphs? did it lose something the old version was doing?). Give ONE next step based on where the essay is NOW, not where it was before. Quote both old and new versions. Mention word economy if the revision changed length.

**Failure modes**:
- Re-diagnoses the whole essay instead of responding to the revision
- Doesn't name the craft shift — just says "this is better"
- Doesn't check for broken connections to other paragraphs
- Gives a list of remaining issues instead of ONE next step
- Doesn't reference the prior coaching context

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 8 (development path — clear next step), Dimension 3 (structural diagnosis — catches broken connections), Dimension 11 (memory — references prior discussion), Dimension 16 (honest assessment — accurate quality calibration of the revision). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 4: Student Resists Feedback

**Essay**: The piano essay.

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think of my essay?"
2. (Coach identifies the summary-mode problem and suggests scene-based alternatives)
3. "Actually I meant the music-coding parallel to show my versatility. I don't think it needs scenes."

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 3: Diagnose the resistance type — is this Type 1 (they see something the coach doesn't) or Type 2 (they're protecting something)? The student says the essay is about "versatility" — evaluate whether the TEXT actually communicates versatility. Quote the specific passages (P4: "parallels with another interest: coding") and assess: does the reader experience versatility, or just a surface analogy? Be honest: the text says these things are similar, it doesn't show the student being versatile. But honor the student's intent — if versatility is what they want to convey, show them what that would actually look like in the writing.

**Failure modes**:
- Capitulates entirely ("Oh, I see what you mean, let me reconsider")
- Doubles down aggressively ("No, you need scenes, trust me")
- Ignores the resistance and continues with original advice
- Accepts the student's framing without evaluating it against the text
- Doesn't quote specific text to support the assessment
- Argues about interpretation rather than evaluating text against intent

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 12 (emotional intelligence — reads resistance type correctly), Dimension 16 (honest assessment — evaluates text against intent), Dimension 9 (agency — honors the student's intent while being honest about execution). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 5: Student Is Confused

**Essay**: The piano essay.

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think?"
2. (Coach gives structural analysis with technique names)
3. "I'm not sure I understand what you mean by 'structural spine' or 'through-line.' Can you explain more simply?"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 3: Change modality immediately. Don't re-explain the same concepts with more words — SHOW. Quote two specific passages from the essay and ask the student what THEY see. Or write a brief demonstration that makes the abstract concept concrete. Limit to one concept per response. Drop craft vocabulary entirely — use plain language. The student's confusion is a signal that the coach's approach needs to change, not that the coach needs to explain louder.

**Failure modes**:
- Repeats the same explanation in slightly different words
- Adds MORE craft vocabulary and technical terms
- Patronizes ("Let me break this down for you in simpler terms")
- Ignores the confusion and moves to a different topic
- Gives a dictionary-style definition of "structural spine"

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 12 (emotional intelligence — recognizes confusion signal), Dimension 9 (agency — empowers through showing rather than explaining), Dimension 4 (sentence-level craft — demonstrates rather than describes). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 6: Student Has a Breakthrough

**Essay**: The piano essay.

**Student messages**:
1-4. (Prior turns establishing issues, collecting context about hackathon and Mrs. Chen)
5. "I just realized something — this whole essay is really about the moment I first heard Mrs. Chen play a Chopin Nocturne. That's when I felt like anything was possible."

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 5: Name the breakthrough ("You just named your essay's emotional core"). Keep the response SHORT — 3-5 sentences max. Don't dilute the moment with extensive analysis. Connect the breakthrough to the immediate next step ("Now write the first three sentences of that scene — your hands on the keys, her hands just left them"). Don't celebrate excessively. Don't add caveats or complications. The student just found the thing — help them hold it and start writing from it.

**Failure modes**:
- Long response that dilutes the breakthrough with analysis
- Adds complications ("But you still need to decide about the hackathon...")
- Celebrates excessively rather than channeling the momentum
- Doesn't connect the breakthrough to a specific writing action
- Uses the breakthrough as an opportunity to re-diagnose the essay
- Doesn't name what the student just discovered in craft terms

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 12 (emotional intelligence — reads breakthrough correctly), Dimension 8 (development path — channels the momentum immediately), Dimension 19 (multi-turn coherence — this turn connects prior threads). Target: 9+ average. NOTE: breakthrough response MUST be under 1000 characters to score above 7.

### Scenario 7: Strong Essay

**Essay**: An essay that's already at the architecture/craft phase — strong specific opening, clear identity, good scenes, voice is working. The student asks: "What do you think?"

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think of my essay?"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 1: Acknowledge the strength specifically — not "this is great" but "P1 drops the reader into a specific moment and earns the transition to P2. The voice in P3 is distinctive — 'I kept the left hand on Chopin's bass line' is the kind of specificity that only you could write." Then identify the ONE refinement that would move it from "admits" to "remembers." Don't over-diagnose. Don't find problems that aren't there. If the essay is at polish level, give polish-level advice.

**Failure modes**:
- Over-diagnoses a strong essay as if it were weak
- Lists 5 improvement areas for an essay that needs 1
- Sycophantic without specificity ("This is amazing!")
- Gives foundation-level advice to a polish-level essay
- Doesn't acknowledge what's working before suggesting refinements

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 16 (honest assessment — calibrates correctly for a strong essay), Dimension 14 (word economy — respects that the essay is tight), Dimension 20 (competitive differentiation — pushes from "admits" to "remembers"). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 8: AI-Convergence Language

**Essay**: An essay that uses AI-convergence patterns: "This transformative experience profoundly impacted my multifaceted journey of personal growth. The tapestry of my experiences wove together to create a rich narrative of resilience and innovation."

**Student messages**:
1. "I got some help from ChatGPT polishing my essay. What do you think?"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 1: Name the convergence language specifically — quote the phrases that read as AI-generated. Explain WHY this is a problem in admissions context (AOs can identify AI language; it makes the essay sound like every other AI-polished essay; it erases the student's authentic voice). Show what the student's material sounds like WITHOUT the AI polish — find the most authentic sentence in the essay and contrast it with the most convergence-prone sentence. Don't shame the student for using AI. Demonstrate what their own voice sounds like and why it's more valuable.

**Failure modes**:
- Doesn't identify the AI-convergence language
- Shames the student for using ChatGPT
- Says "it sounds a bit polished" without naming specific phrases
- Doesn't contrast AI voice with the student's authentic voice
- Suggests "use more specific details" without demonstrating the voice difference
- Treats this as a style issue rather than a competitive survival issue

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 10 (anti-convergence — catches AI language), Dimension 7 (voice matching — identifies the student's authentic register), Dimension 5 (reader psychology — explains the AO's response to AI language), Dimension 6 (demonstration — shows what the student's own voice sounds like). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 9: Student Asks About a Specific Paragraph

**Essay**: The piano essay.

**Student messages**:
1. "What do you think?"
2. "Can you help me with paragraph 3? The voice feels different from the rest of the essay."

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 2: Focus on P3 specifically. Use the deep analysis — P3 is the Chopin-jazz paragraph, and the student is right that it sounds different. Diagnose WHY: P3 is closer to the work than P1 (gerunds of action vs. nouns of summary). But P3 is still in summary mode — "I blended them with contemporary jazz rhythms" describes the fusion without putting the reader at the keyboard. Connect P3's role to the essay's architecture. Explain what P3 would need to do to fulfill its structural role. Ask for the specific compositional details that would make P3 come alive.

**Failure modes**:
- Ignores the scope request and coaches the whole essay
- Doesn't validate the student's observation ("you're right, it IS different")
- Gives generic paragraph advice without referencing P3's specific text
- Doesn't explain WHY P3 sounds different (proximity to work)
- Doesn't connect P3 to the essay's overall architecture
- Doesn't quote specific P3 text

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 3 (structural diagnosis — P3's role in architecture), Dimension 4 (sentence-level craft — why the voice is different at the sentence level), Dimension 9 (agency — validates the student's observation), Dimension 11 (memory — builds on turn 1). Target: 8+ average.

### Scenario 10: Late Session — Revision Plan Needed

**Essay**: The piano essay after 8 turns of coaching. The student has shared the hackathon context, Mrs. Chen, the Chopin moment. The session has explored structure, voice, and multiple paragraphs. Now:

**Student messages**:
9. "We've talked about a lot. What should I do first when I sit down to revise?"

**Expected coaching behavior**:
- Turn 9: Consolidate. The student needs three things: (1) a clear first revision task with specific constraints ("Rewrite P1 using the hackathon scene. 160 words max. Start with a physical action."), (2) the ONE principle that matters most for their essay ("Every paragraph needs to put the reader in a room, not explain a concept"), (3) a prioritized list of what to tackle in what order. Do NOT introduce new issues. Do NOT write more sample prose. Help them leave knowing exactly what to do first.

**Failure modes**:
- Introduces new issues at turn 9 when the student needs consolidation
- Gives a comprehensive list without prioritization
- Writes more sample prose instead of giving a revision plan
- Doesn't connect the revision plan to specific conversations from earlier turns
- Gives abstract principles without concrete tasks
- Doesn't include word count or paragraph-level constraints

**Scoring criteria**: Dimension 18 (revision readiness — concrete plan), Dimension 19 (multi-turn coherence — consolidation, not new diagnosis), Dimension 14 (word economy — plan includes cuts), Dimension 8 (development path — actionable constraints). Target: 9+ average.

## C. COMPARATIVE BENCHMARKING

For each coaching situation, here is what different tiers of coaches would do, and where our system should land relative to each.

### 1. Elite Human Consultant ($500/hr — Ivy Coach tier)

**What they do that our system must match**:
- Read the essay once and identify the PERSON, not just the text. They build a theory of who this student is within 30 seconds.
- Ask the student questions that surface the real essay hidden inside the submitted one. "Tell me about Mrs. Chen" is more important than "your opening needs work."
- Write demonstrations in the student's voice — not polished consultant prose but the student's best register amplified.
- Manage the session arc deliberately. They know when to diagnose, when to explore, when to demonstrate, and when to send the student home with a plan.
- Say "this essay is mediocre" kindly but clearly. They don't inflate or hedge.
- Know exactly what's in the admissions pile because they've been in the committee room (or coached hundreds of students who were).

**What they do that our system CANNOT currently match**:
- Read the student's face, posture, and vocal tone in real-time. They see the flinch when they name a weakness, the excitement when they hit a nerve.
- Draw on 20 years of specific admissions outcomes. "I had a student write almost exactly this essay for Stanford last year. Here's what the AO said."
- Know the specific admissions officer who might read this essay and what they value.
- Build rapport over weeks — the coaching relationship has depth that a single session cannot.

**Our system's advantage over them**:
- Deep structural analysis at a level of precision most humans can't maintain (paragraph-level architectural mapping, sentence-level craft diagnosis across the entire essay simultaneously).
- Perfect memory across the session — no thread dropped, no detail forgotten.
- Anti-convergence detection at scale — we've ingested the convergence patterns that a single consultant might not notice because they haven't read 10,000 essays this month.
- Consistent quality — no bad days, no running late, no phoning it in on a Wednesday afternoon.

### 2. Good Human Tutor ($100/hr — English teacher moonlighting)

**What they do well**:
- Care about the student genuinely.
- Can explain basic essay concepts (thesis, structure, evidence).
- Read the essay carefully and give honest feedback.

**What they miss that our system catches**:
- Archetype saturation — they've read 50 essays, not 50,000. They don't know the music-to-coding pipeline is a cliche because they haven't seen it 200 times.
- Sentence-level craft at the level of named techniques. They know "this doesn't sound right" but not "this sentence is doing summary work when it should be doing scene work because the verb is gerund-as-description rather than gerund-as-action."
- AO reading psychology — how exhaustion, pile position, and committee dynamics affect the essay's competitive position.
- The difference between "good essay" and "admissions-competitive essay."

**What they do that our system should learn from**:
- They naturally adjust to the student's energy level because they can feel it.
- They use silence — "just sit and think for a minute" — because they're in the room.
- They give warmth naturally, not as a prompt instruction.

### 3. Generic AI Coach (ChatGPT + "help me with my essay")

**What they do**:
- Give general essay advice drawn from common knowledge.
- List 5-7 improvement areas without prioritization.
- May rewrite passages in polished AI prose.
- Treat every essay the same way.

**What our system does that they cannot**:
- Deep essay-specific analysis (11-dimension understanding, paragraph-level architectural mapping, sentence-level voice diagnosis).
- Session memory that builds across turns — not a stateless Q&A.
- Anti-convergence: generic AI coaching CREATES convergence (pushes every essay toward the same template). Our system detects and counters it.
- Admissions-specific grounding — AO simulation, archetype detection, put-down risk assessment.
- Demonstration quality — sample prose that matches the student's voice rather than generic "improved" prose.
- Phase-aware coaching — Foundation essays get different coaching than Polish essays.
- Resistance handling with diagnostic sophistication — three resistance types, not just "let me try again."
- Session arc management — knows when to diagnose, when to explore, when to demonstrate, when to consolidate.
- Word economy integration — never suggests additions without cuts.
- Student theory building — accumulates understanding of who the student IS, not just what they wrote.

**Where generic AI beats us (and shouldn't)**:
- Response time. ChatGPT responds in 2 seconds; our system takes 15-30 seconds per turn. This is acceptable but we should never be slower than 30 seconds.
- Accessibility of language. ChatGPT defaults to approachable language; our system sometimes uses craft vocabulary before the student is ready.

## D. GRADING SCALE

### Outcome-Based Grade Definitions

Each grade describes what the ESSAY would look like after a full coaching session, what the STUDENT would experience, and what the coaching FEELS like.

**A+ (9.0-10.0)** — THE STANDARD. Every session should target this.:
- ESSAY OUTCOME: The essay that results from this coaching would be competitive at Harvard, Stanford, MIT. It reveals an unmistakable person. An AO would bring it up in committee. The voice is singular. The structure earns every word. This is the baseline goal for EVERY student, regardless of where they started.
- STUDENT EXPERIENCE: The student discovered something about themselves they didn't know before. They leave with transferable writing skills — not just a better essay, but a better understanding of how writing works. They feel like a writer, not a student being corrected.
- COACHING QUALITY: Indistinguishable from the best human consultant. Every turn advances the conversation. No wasted words. The coach understands this essay as deeply as the student does and sees things the student can't. The student would choose this over a $500/hr human.
- DIMENSIONAL PROFILE: 9+ on at least 16 of 20 dimensions. No dimension below 7.

**A (8.5-8.9)** — GOOD BUT NOT THE STANDARD:
- ESSAY OUTCOME: The essay would be competitive at top-20 schools. Strong identity, clear voice, distinctive moments. Close to committee-level but missing the last degree of precision or originality that makes an AO stop and re-read.
- STUDENT EXPERIENCE: Productive and energizing. The student knows what to revise and why. Some moments felt more like receiving instructions than co-creating, but the overall arc was strong.
- COACHING QUALITY: Better than most human consultants. Clear strengths with 2-3 noticeable gaps — perhaps one demonstration slightly off-voice, one turn that went broad instead of deep, or the coach wrote more than the student did.
- DIMENSIONAL PROFILE: 8+ on at least 14 of 20 dimensions. No dimension below 6.

**B+ (8.0-8.4)**:
- ESSAY OUTCOME: The essay would be decent at competitive schools. Improved from where it started but missing the "only this student could write this" quality.
- STUDENT EXPERIENCE: Helpful but not transformative. The student knows their essay has problems and has some ideas for fixing them, but doesn't feel the creative excitement of discovering their essay's real subject.
- COACHING QUALITY: Competent but generic in places. Several dimensions are at the B level — maybe the coach gave template advice, didn't catch convergence language, or treated every turn with the same intensity.
- DIMENSIONAL PROFILE: 7+ on at least 14 of 20 dimensions. Multiple dimensions at 5-6.

**B (7.0-7.9)**:
- ESSAY OUTCOME: The essay would be adequate. Fixed some obvious problems but still reads as a version of a common essay type.
- STUDENT EXPERIENCE: The student received useful feedback but could have gotten similar advice from a Google search for "college essay tips."
- COACHING QUALITY: Significant gaps. The coaching lists problems without demonstrating solutions, doesn't build across turns, gives generic advice, or doesn't respect the student's agency.
- DIMENSIONAL PROFILE: Mixed — some dimensions at 7-8, many at 4-6.

**C or below (<7.0)**:
- ESSAY OUTCOME: The coaching made the essay worse, more generic, or more confused.
- STUDENT EXPERIENCE: The student is more confused or less confident than before. The coaching may have been sycophantic (false confidence), overly critical (crushed confidence), or contradictory (confused).
- COACHING QUALITY: Harmful. Actively pushes toward convergence. Ignores the student's intent. Ghostwrites. Gives wrong advice.
- DIMENSIONAL PROFILE: Multiple dimensions below 5.

## E. SPECIFIC FAILURE MODES TO DETECT (25)

Each failure mode is an observable behavior that, if detected, indicates the system is NOT performing at A+ level. Failure modes are organized by severity.

### Critical Failures (automatic grade cap at B+ if present)

**F1: Sycophantic Opening**
The coach leads with encouragement before any insight. "Great question!" / "There's a lot to like here!" / "I can tell you worked hard on this."
- *Detection*: First sentence contains a compliment, encouragement, or frame before substance.
- *Why it matters*: Students don't need to feel good. They need to get better. Opening with a compliment wastes the most valuable sentence in the response and signals that the coach is managing feelings rather than teaching craft.

**F2: Ghostwriting**
The coach writes more than 4 sentences of sample prose in a single turn, or writes sample prose more than twice in a session without asking the student to write their own version.
- *Detection*: Count demonstration sentences per turn and per session. Check for "now you try" or equivalent after each demonstration.
- *Why it matters*: The goal is a developed writer, not a ghostwritten essay. The student must leave having written their OWN sentences.

**F3: Convergence Coaching**
Every suggestion pushes the essay toward the "scene opening -> tension -> reflection -> growth" template, regardless of what the essay is trying to do.
- *Detection*: Check whether the coach's suggestions honor the essay's existing strengths or impose a formula. Does the coach suggest a scene opening for an essay that works better as analytical?
- *Why it matters*: AI coaching creates convergence zones where every essay sounds the same. The coach's job is the opposite — amplify what's unique.

**F4: Fabricated Details**
The coach's demonstration uses details the student never shared — made-up names, invented moments, fictional reactions.
- *Detection*: Cross-reference demonstration details against studentDeclaredContext and conversation history.
- *Why it matters*: Fabricated demonstrations are useless — the student can't build on details that aren't theirs. They also erode trust.

**F5: Ignoring Student Corrections**
When the student says "actually, P3 is about Mrs. Chen, not innovation," the coach doesn't update their understanding.
- *Detection*: Check whether the coach's subsequent responses integrate the student's correction or continue with the pre-correction analysis.
- *Why it matters*: The student owns their essay's meaning. A coach who doesn't update on corrections is coaching their own essay, not the student's.

### Major Failures (automatic grade cap at A if present)

**F6: Show Don't Tell Without Showing**
The coach says "show don't tell" or equivalent without demonstrating what "showing" looks like with the student's specific material.
- *Detection*: Search for "show don't tell," "be more specific," "add details" without an accompanying demonstration.
- *Why it matters*: Diagnosis without demonstration is B-level coaching. The student knows their essay "should be more specific" — they need to SEE what specific looks like in THEIR essay.

**F7: Repeating Prior-Turn Advice**
The coach gives the same observation or suggestion that was given in a prior turn, rephrased but not deepened.
- *Detection*: 4-word phrase overlap analysis between the current turn's core advice and all prior turns' core advice. Also semantic similarity check.
- *Why it matters*: If the student returns to a topic, they need a deeper or different angle, not the same observation again. Repetition means the coach has nothing new to offer, which means the coaching session has stalled.

**F8: Revision Non-Response**
The student submits a revision, and the coach doesn't assess the delta — they give fresh diagnosis as if seeing the essay for the first time.
- *Detection*: Check whether the coach references what CHANGED vs. the prior version when responding to a revision.
- *Why it matters*: The student did work. Not acknowledging that work disrespects their effort and fails to teach them whether their instincts were right.

**F9: List of Five Without Prioritization**
The coach gives 5+ suggestions in a single turn without identifying which one matters most.
- *Detection*: Count distinct actionable suggestions per turn. Check for prioritization language ("start with," "most important," "first").
- *Why it matters*: Five unprioritized suggestions is a pamphlet, not coaching. The student doesn't know where to start. ONE deep suggestion with a development path teaches more than five surface suggestions.

**F10: Wrong Phase Coaching**
The coach gives polish-level advice to a Foundation-phase essay, or Foundation-level advice to a Polish-phase essay.
- *Detection*: Compare the improvement phase with the level of coaching advice. Foundation should not include craft vocabulary or sentence-level precision. Polish should not include "what is this essay about?"
- *Why it matters*: Phase-inappropriate coaching is disorienting. A student whose essay doesn't yet have a clear identity doesn't need to hear about comma splices.

### Significant Failures (deduct 0.5 points per occurrence)

**F11: No Text Quotation**
The coach gives advice about the essay without quoting a single specific sentence, phrase, or word from the essay text.
- *Detection*: Check for quoted essay text in the response. At least one direct quote required per substantive response.
- *Why it matters*: Ungrounded advice is generic advice. Quoting the text proves the coach has read THIS essay, not a summary of it.

**F12: Addition Without Cut**
The coach suggests adding content (a scene, a detail, a paragraph) without identifying what to cut to make room within 650 words.
- *Detection*: Check whether addition suggestions are paired with cut suggestions.
- *Why it matters*: The essay is 650 words. Every addition requires a subtraction. Suggesting additions without cuts implies the student has unlimited space.

**F13: Coach Writes, Student Doesn't**
The coach writes sample prose without ever inviting the student to write their own version.
- *Detection*: Check for "now you try," "write me," "your turn," or equivalent after demonstrations.
- *Why it matters*: Demonstrations teach craft. But the student learns by DOING, not by reading the coach's prose.

**F14: Generic Question Ending**
The turn ends with "What do you think?" or "Does that make sense?" instead of a specific question that forces creative action.
- *Detection*: Check the final question against a list of generic closings.
- *Why it matters*: Generic questions invite generic responses. Specific questions ("What were your hands doing when that shifted?") generate the material the coaching needs.

**F15: Process Narration**
The coach narrates their own process instead of delivering substance. "I notice that..." / "Let me share some thoughts on..." / "Looking at your essay, I can see that..."
- *Detection*: Search for first-person process narration in the first 2 sentences.
- *Why it matters*: Process narration wastes the student's attention on the coach's internal state rather than delivering insight.

**F16: Unchanged Strategic Question**
The same strategic question persists for 4+ turns without the coach weaving it into the conversation or updating it.
- *Detection*: Check question staleness counter in session memory.
- *Why it matters*: A stale strategic question means the conversation has stalled or the coach isn't using the question to drive discovery.

**F17: Celebration Overreaction**
When the student has a breakthrough, the coach responds with excessive celebration or a lengthy analysis that dilutes the moment.
- *Detection*: Check response length after breakthrough events. Should be under 1000 characters.
- *Why it matters*: Breakthroughs need momentum, not celebration. The coach should name the insight, connect it to the next step, and stop.

**F18: Student's Words Parroted Back**
The coach uses the student's exact phrasing as a teaching device when what the student needs is new language to replace their current framing.
- *Detection*: Check whether the coach's "reframing" is substantially the same words the student already used.
- *Why it matters*: When a student says "I think it's about versatility," they need to hear "what the essay is actually communicating is X" — not "I hear you saying it's about versatility."

**F19: Interpretation Without Permission**
The coach builds an elaborate interpretation of the essay's "real meaning" and structures all subsequent coaching around it, without checking whether the interpretation matches the student's lived experience.
- *Detection*: Check for projection-checking language ("Does that ring true?" / "Am I reading too much into it?") after interpretive claims.
- *Why it matters*: The student owns their essay's meaning. A brilliant interpretation that doesn't match their experience is useless and alienating.

**F20: Scope Creep in Late Turns**
In turns 7+, the coach introduces new issues rather than consolidating and prioritizing the issues already discussed.
- *Detection*: Check whether late-turn responses introduce new paragraph analyses or dimension discussions not previously covered.
- *Why it matters*: Late sessions should consolidate. New diagnoses at turn 9 leave the student overwhelmed and without a clear revision priority.

### Minor Failures (deduct 0.25 points per occurrence)

**F21: Inconsistent Tone Register**
The coach's voice shifts between turns — casual and direct in one turn, formal and hedging in the next.
- *Detection*: Compare tone markers (contractions, sentence length, hedging language) across turns.
- *Why it matters*: Tonal inconsistency makes the coach feel unreliable.

**F22: Unresolved Thread Accumulation**
The coach opens questions or threads in early turns and never returns to them.
- *Detection*: Track questions asked in each turn and check whether they're addressed in subsequent turns.
- *Why it matters*: Unresolved threads suggest the coach loses track of the conversation.

**F23: Missed Pattern Recognition**
The student exhibits a behavioral pattern across turns (e.g., always deflecting from structural questions) that the coach doesn't name.
- *Detection*: Check learning style observations for pattern recognition and whether patterns are surfaced in coaching.
- *Why it matters*: Naming patterns is a coaching superpower. It helps the student see their own behavior.

**F24: Demonstration Without Craft Name**
The coach writes sample prose without naming the craft move being demonstrated.
- *Detection*: Check whether demonstrations are preceded or followed by a technique name.
- *Why it matters*: Unnamed demonstrations teach by osmosis. Named demonstrations teach transferable craft.

**F25: Same Format Every Turn**
Every coaching turn uses the same format and length regardless of the student's state and needs.
- *Detection*: Compare response lengths and formats across turns. Check for variation in response intensity.
- *Why it matters*: A breakthrough needs 3 sentences. A deep diagnosis needs a full response. A writing prompt needs one sentence. Same-format responses ignore the moment's needs.

## F. SCORING METHODOLOGY

### Per-Turn Scoring

For each turn, score all 20 dimensions that are applicable (not all dimensions apply to every turn — Dimension 19 doesn't apply to turn 1, for example). Record:
- Dimension scores (1-10)
- Applicable failure modes detected (F1-F25)
- Failure mode deductions

**Per-turn composite score** = (average of applicable dimension scores) - (sum of failure mode deductions), clamped to [1.0, 10.0].

### Per-Session Scoring

**Session composite score** = weighted average of per-turn composite scores, with the following turn weights:
- Turn 1: 1.5x (first impression establishes trust and quality bar)
- Turns 2-3: 1.0x (standard)
- Turn 4+ if breakthrough: 1.5x (breakthrough handling is a signature moment)
- Turn 4+ if revision response: 1.2x (revision coaching is a key differentiator)
- Final turn: 1.3x (consolidation quality determines what the student takes away)
- All other turns: 1.0x

### Grade Assignment

Apply the grade scale from Section D using the session composite score. Then check for grade caps from critical failure modes (Section E).

### Confidence Intervals

For a meaningful evaluation, minimum 3 turns are required. For full-framework evaluation, 8-10 turns recommended. Single-turn evaluations should be labeled "partial" and used for dimension-level feedback only, not overall grading.

## G. APPLYING THIS FRAMEWORK

### For Automated Evaluation

1. For each coaching turn, extract the response text and the full session context (essay text, conversation history, session memory, student declared context).
2. Score each applicable dimension using the rubric anchors.
3. Detect failure modes using the specified detection methods.
4. Compute per-turn and per-session composite scores.
5. Assign grade using the grade scale and failure mode caps.

### For Human Evaluation (Calibration)

1. Three evaluators independently score the same coaching session.
2. Discuss and resolve disagreements >2 points on any dimension.
3. Compute inter-rater reliability. Target: Krippendorff's alpha > 0.7 per dimension.
4. Use consensus scores for official evaluation.

### For A/B Testing

1. Hold the essay, student messages, and turn count constant.
2. Generate coaching responses from System A and System B.
3. Score both using this framework.
4. Report dimension-level deltas and composite score deltas.
5. Statistical significance: minimum 20 scenarios, paired t-test on composite scores.
