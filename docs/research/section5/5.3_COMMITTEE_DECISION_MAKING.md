## Overview and Key Findings

### Executive Summary

Committee decision-making is where **borderline applicants' fates are determined**. The key insight: admissions is **advocacy, not evaluation**. Officers must "pitch" their applicants, and the quality of that pitch—combined with institutional priorities—determines outcomes for the ~30% of applicants who are genuinely competitive.

### Critical Findings

| Finding | Source | Implication |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| First reader gatekeeping: ~20% never reach committee | SFFA data | First read is decisive |
| Borderline cases: 10-30 min vs 2-5 min for clear cases | Committee research | Borderline = high attention |
| Dean's List: 7x more likely to admit | Harvard litigation | Development cases matter |
| Recruited Athletes: 14x more likely | SFFA analysis | Athletic hooks are dominant |
| Legacy: 5.7x more likely | NBER research | Legacy provides significant boost |
| Geographic diversity: PRIMARY tipping factor | College Transitions | Where you're from matters |
| 46% of votes change after deliberation | Academic Medicine | Discussion influences outcomes |

## School-Specific Committee Architectures

### Harvard's Three-Stage Model

**Structure:**

| Stage | Participants | Size | Function |
|-------|-------------|------|----------|
| **Stage 1** | First reader | 1 | Assigns ratings, creates sheet |
| **Stage 2** | Regional subcommittee | 5-8 | AOs, faculty, senior staff chair |
| **Stage 3** | Full committee | ~40 | Final authority |

**Gatekeeping Rules:**

| Rating | Path |
|--------|------|
| **2- or better** | AUTO-ADVANCES to subcommittee |
| **3** | First reader DISCRETION |
| **3+ to 6** | ~20% never reach committee |

**Dean's Authority:**
- William Fitzsimmons maintains "Dean's Interest List"
- Can "pre-emptively join" meetings
- Discuss applicants with subcommittee chairs beforehand
- Decisions can be revisited "virtually until the day acceptance letters are mailed"

**Source:** [Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/10/18/day-three-harvard-admissions-trial/)

### Penn's Consensus Model

> "We go around [the table] and together by CONSENSUS decide who will be admitted."
> — **Dean Lee Stetson**, Penn

**Key Features:**
- 19 AOs assigned to 15 U.S. regions, 4 international, 1 transfers
- Regional officer = applicant's "mentor" and "champion"
- Consensus (not majority) requires genuine agreement
- 5-4 votes don't count as consensus

**Source:** Penn Admissions documentation, [LogicPrep](https://www.logicprep.com/blog/inside-admissions-how-do-admission-committees-work)

### Penn's Committee-Based Evaluation (CBE)

**Innovation:** Paired-reading methodology
- Two AOs sit together in same room
- Read application simultaneously
- Discuss impressions
- Reach consensus BEFORE file goes to committee

**Benefits:**
- Reduces individual subjectivity
- Guards against "bad day" reading
- Leverages different expertise

### MIT's "Redundantly Subjective" Review

> "When a student has been admitted to MIT, it means that **just about everyone in the office thinks they are awesome**."
> — [MIT Admissions](https://mitadmissions.org/)

**Structure:**
- Three cohorts: 6,000 EA, 8,000 domestic RD, 4,000 international RD
- 4-6 weeks of full-time reading per cohort
- Each app gets minimum one read; many get multiple
- Dual-committee structure:
  1. Selection committee identifies strong candidates
  2. Higher-level committee confirms final decisions

### Wesleyan's Time-Limited Model (Counterpoint)

**Historical process:**
- 5 minutes per applicant discussion
- Immediate vote

**Problems:**
- Privileges clear-cut cases
- Borderline calls become "essentially random"
- Illustrates how process design impacts equity

## Special Approval Pathways (ALDC)

### The ALDC Framework

| Letter | Category | Odds Multiplier | Admission Rate | Share of Admits |
|--------|----------|-----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **A** | Athletes (Recruited) | **14x** | ~86% | ~12-20% |
| **L** | Legacies | **5.7x** | ~34% | ~10-15% |
| **D** | Dean's Interest | **7x** | ~42% | ~5% |
| **C** | Children of Faculty/Staff | ~2.5x | ~47% | ~5% |

**Total ALDC share of Harvard admits: 43%**

**Source:** SFFA v. Harvard litigation, [NBER Working Paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26316/w26316.pdf)

### Dean's Interest List Details

> "Applicants end up on the 'Dean's Interest List,' which includes individuals the dean wants to keep track of as they move through the admissions process. It includes the children of alumni and donors, who were scored based on how important their connection was to Harvard."
> — [Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/10/18/day-three-harvard-admissions-trial/)

**Statistics:**
- 42.2% admission rate for Dean's List members (vs ~6% overall)
- In 2019, 192 Harvard seniors (>10% of class) had been on the list
- Nearly 60% of Z-List students were also on Dean's Interest List

**Threshold:** A major donation to Harvard is $10 million or more—from someone with an established relationship (not new donors whose children are applying).

**Source:** [BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/mollyhensleyclancy/harvard-donors-admissions)

### Recruited Athletes

> "Athletic recruitment operates through parallel process. Committee DEFERS to athletic department. Coaching staff recommendations 'effectively determine outcomes.'"

**Statistics (Harvard):**
- Athletes with Academic 1 or 2: **83% admit rate** (vs 16% non-athletes)
- Athletes with Academic 4: **70.5% admit rate** (vs 0.076% non-athletes)
- Athletes comprise 15-20% of undergraduate population despite 5% overall admit rate

**Source:** [Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/), [BestColleges](https://www.bestcolleges.com/news/analysis/athletic-recruitment-at-elite-colleges-favors-wealthy-white-students/)

### Legacy Applicants

> "From 2014 to 2019, Harvard legacies enjoyed a **33.6% admit rate**, more than 5 times higher than the general admission rate of 5.9%."
> — [Ivy Coach](https://www.ivycoach.com/the-ivy-coach-blog/admissions-process/legacy-admission-at-harvard/)

**School-Specific Legacy Rates:**
- Princeton: Over 30% legacy admit rate (vs <5% overall)
- Penn (Early Decision): 41.7% legacy admit rate
- Brown (2006): 33.5% legacy admit rate

**Post-SFFA Status:** As of 2024, no Ivy League school has eliminated legacy admissions despite 75% of Americans opposing the practice.

**Source:** [Legacy Preferences Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_preferences), [Ivy Coach](https://www.ivycoach.com/)

## Borderline Case Tipping Factors

### 1. Geographic Diversity (PRIMARY)

> "When all other factors are equal among borderline applicants, colleges consistently favor 'the applicant coming from a more distant or exotic locale.'"
> — [College Transitions](https://www.collegetransitions.com/blog/geographic-diversity-and-college-admissions/)

**Why It Matters:**
- Perceived selectivity (national recruitment improves metrics)
- Revenue diversity (out-of-state students)
- Cultural/intellectual diversity
- "All 50 states" bragging rights

**Advantaged Regions:**
- Montana, Wyoming, West Virginia, North Dakota
- Rural areas anywhere
- States with few elite college aspirants

**Disadvantaged Regions:**
- Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, California
- Wealthy suburban metros
- Areas with high elite college application density

**Post-SFFA Intensification:**
> "Following the June 2023 Supreme Court decision, elite universities have intensified geographic recruitment."
> — [U.S. News](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/articles/how-your-hometown-could-affect-your-college-prospects)

### 2. Class Composition Balancing

| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| **Major balance** | Too many pre-meds? Philosophy gets boost |
| **International balance** | Active diversity within constraints |
| **Socioeconomic diversity** | Pell-eligible, first-gen students boosted |
| **Gender balance** | Underrepresented gender in STEM boosted |

### 3. Demonstrated Fit and Mission Alignment

> "Applicants who convincingly demonstrate why the specific institution aligns with their intellectual and personal goals often receive borderline boosts."

**Requirements:**
- Genuine knowledge of institution
- Authentic articulation of "why this school"
- NOT generic statements applicable to many schools

### 4. First-Generation Status

> "All of America's elite universities covet first-generation college students because they see their institutions as channels to America's meritocracy."
> — [Ivy Coach](https://www.ivycoach.com/the-ivy-coach-blog/college-admissions/first-gen-college-students-advantage/)

**First-Gen Representation:**
- Harvard: ~15% first-generation
- Duke: ~10% first-generation

**Benefit:** "When all other factors are the same, being first generation might be the tipping point for admissions or scholarships."

### 5. Exceptional Narrative or Resilience

> "An applicant with a 3.6 GPA who built that from a freshman-year crisis may receive favorable reconsideration compared to an applicant with a straight 3.8 who demonstrates no growth trajectory."

**Valued Character Traits:**
- Resilience
- Growth mindset
- Overcoming challenges
- Self-awareness
- Adaptability

## Officer Advocacy Mechanics

### The Storytelling Role

> "When your file goes to committee, the admissions officer who read it must be able to tell your story in just a few sentences."
> — [Educational Advocates](https://www.educationaladvocates.com/inside-the-admissions-committee/)

**Example Pitches:**
- "My first-generation rancher from Wyoming who trains horses and wrote a compelling essay about perseverance"
- "My aspiring mathematician from Boston who took every advanced course offered, started a math club, and earned glowing recommendations"

### How Officers Choose Who to Champion

> "I only brought cases to committee that I was excited about, that stood out, and that I thought would make my institution better."
> — Anonymous AO, [Collegewise](https://go.collegewise.com/admissions-committees-behind-the-scenes)

**Officer Advocacy Functions:**
1. Explain what they see in the file
2. Frame information for committee
3. Shape what information members focus on
4. Influence how committee interprets ambiguous evidence

### Making Applications "Easy to Advocate For"

> "Your job as an applicant is to give them something memorable and authentic to advocate for. If your file feels safe, generic, or incomplete, it is much harder for them to fight for you in a competitive room."
> — [Educational Advocates](https://www.educationaladvocates.com/inside-the-admissions-committee/)

**Practical Example:**
> "One former Vanderbilt admissions officer remembers a student who ran a free virtual summer science camp for elementary students during the COVID summer of 2020. She led the young scientists in DIY home experiments to teach them about the scientific method. That is pretty much exactly what the Vanderbilt Student Volunteers for Science (VSVS) club does. So, the admissions officer told the committee that they were presenting them with 'the rare opportunity to admit the future president of VSVS'... and they did!"
> — [Polygence](https://www.polygence.org/blog/advocating-for-passion-projects-for-admissions)

## Voting and Consensus Systems

### Harvard's Voting System

- **Subcommittee:** Simple majority to advance
- **Full committee:** Simple majority for admission
- **Reversibility:** Full committee CAN revisit and REVERSE earlier decisions

### Penn's Consensus Requirement

> "We go around the table and together by consensus decide who will be admitted."
> — Dean Lee Stetson

**Implications:**
- More discussion and deliberation required
- Disagreements resolved through PERSUASION
- Cannot reach consensus through bare 5-4 votes

### Yale's Voting Threshold

> "Any applicant with more than a total of two reject and/or wait-list votes was automatically denied."
> — Ivy College Prep

**Threshold Rules:**
- **3+ affirmative votes** → Guaranteed admission consideration
- **2+ negative votes** → Automatic denial

### Variation in Systems

| System | Schools | Characteristics |
|--------|---------|-----------------|
| **Simple Majority** | Harvard | Quick but potentially divisive |
| **Consensus** | Penn | Thorough but time-intensive |
| **Unanimous** | Some elite schools | Highest bar, ensures broad support |
| **Threshold Rules** | Yale | Automatic cutoffs reduce deliberation |

## Reader Disagreement and Escalation

### The Escalation Principle

When first and second readers disagree:
- Application FLAGGED for closer examination
- NOT averaged or split
- Disagreement signals "interpretive complexity"

### Resolution Mechanisms

| Disagreement Type | Resolution |
|-------------------|------------|
| Minor (1 point) | Usually defaults to first reader |
| Significant (2+ points) | Third reader or senior review |
| Opposite recommendations | Escalated to dean level |

### Deliberation Impact

> "Research on medical school committees shows that deliberation changes voting in approximately **46% of applications with initial vote disagreement**."
> — [Academic Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12058553/)

## The "Feeling in the Room"

### What It Means

> "Committee decisions are made 'by vote, consensus, or a **FEELING IN THE ROOM**.'"
> — [InGenius Prep](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/college-admissions-officers/)

**Components:**
- Emotional resonance
- Narrative coherence
- Collective intuition about contribution to community

### Why It's Not Irrational

> "This is NOT irrational—it's holistic judgment incorporating character and fit that can't be reduced to rating scales."

**What Creates Positive "Feeling":**
- Clear, memorable narrative
- Authentic voice
- Evidence of potential contribution
- Alignment with institutional values
- Strong officer advocacy

## Recommendations in Committee

### Strong Recommendation Characteristics

| Characteristic | Example |
|----------------|---------|
| **Specific anecdotes** | Concrete examples, not generic praise |
| **Contextual comparison** | "In my 20 years, Sarah is top 5" |
| **Evidence of growth** | Not just high grades |
| **Student as person** | Not just academic performer |

### Red Flags in Recommendations

| Red Flag | Signal |
|----------|--------|
| Generic language | Lack of genuine familiarity |
| Inflated praise without substantiation | Empty endorsement |
| Wrong student name | Carelessness or confusion |
| Brevity | Limited knowledge |
| Hedged language | "seems to be" vs "is" |

### MIT's Recommender Tracking System

> "MIT maintains longitudinal data on the predictive validity of individual recommenders' assessments."

**How It Works:**
- High-credibility recommenders (past recs correlate with student success) receive SPECIAL WEIGHT
- Recommenders with poor track records face DOWNWEIGHTED recommendations

**Implication:** Recommender credibility is institutional knowledge that affects weight of future recommendations.

    enthusiasm: 'strong' | 'moderate' | 'weak' | 'none';
    presentationQuality: number; // 0-100

  | 'explain_file'
