## Overview

Elite admissions evaluates achievements RELATIVE to available opportunities, not in absolute terms. Context fundamentally shapes how activities are assessed.

### Key Takeaways

- **Family responsibilities are "the CORNERSTONE"** - can outweigh perfect test scores
- **Work is POSITIVE, not neutral** - more hours = better, especially manual labor
- **School context matters** - evaluated against similar schools, not all applicants
- **Geographic advantage exists** - "sparse country" recruitment intensified
- **Information asymmetry** - under-resourced students often don't know what's valued

> "Given unequal educational opportunity, it is incumbent upon admissions [officers] to strive to understand the conditions under which each applicant has performed and to make judgments based on the context of those conditions."
> — Jerome A. Lucido

---

## The Core Principle: Contextual Review

### Foundational Insight

Our system must FIRST assess what opportunities were available to the student, THEN evaluate achievement relative to those opportunities.

### Context Categories

1. **Socioeconomic** - Family income, first-generation status
2. **Geographic** - Rural vs urban, school quality
3. **Family obligations** - Work, caregiving, household management
4. **School resources** - AP availability, club offerings, counselor ratio
5. **Health/personal** - Documented circumstances affecting participation

## Paid Work Valuation

### Explicit Institutional Value

| Source | Guidance | Implication |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Sarah Harberson (Penn/Wesleyan AO) | "The more hours, the better, especially for the summer" | Work quantity is a POSITIVE, not neutral |
| Common App | "Family Responsibilities" = dedicated dropdown category | Work is institutionally recognized |
| Washington University | "Students who work for pay...should include this in their activity list" | Explicit directive to include |
| UT Austin (CDS) | "Work experience" = "Important" | Formal weight in rubric |
| UCLA (CDS) | Work experience = "Considered" | Part of evaluation |

### Manual Labor Hierarchy

> "Cleaning toilets, dishwashing, farm work, lawn care...physically harder than an office job, and they give students perspective on the regular working adult"
> — Sarah Harberson

**Manual labor > office work** for character demonstration

### Work Hours Assessment

| Hours/Week | Assessment |
|------------|------------|
| 5-10 | Meaningful, worth listing |
| 10-20 | Significant, equivalent to major EC |
| 20-30 | Substantial, explains reduced other activities |
| 30+ | Exceptional, near-full-time responsibility |

**Implementation:** Work should be evaluated POSITIVELY, not as absence of traditional activities.

## Family Responsibilities: The Cornerstone

### The Most Powerful Statement in All Research

> "Family responsibilities are the **CORNERSTONE of college admissions** because they reveal loyalty, sacrifice, and the love of family a student embodies. **Nothing is more important than that, not even a perfect test score or being #1 in your class.**"
> — Sarah Harberson (Former Penn & F&M Dean)

This is EXTRAORDINARY - family responsibilities can outweigh academic metrics.

### Hour Thresholds for Family Responsibilities

**Minimum threshold:** 4+ hours per week (Harvard Making Caring Common)

| Hours/Week | Assessment | Examples |
|------------|------------|----------|
| **4-9 hours** | Meaningful - worth listing | After-school sibling care, regular household support |
| **10-15 hours** | Significant - strong activity | Daily caregiving responsibilities, consistent elder care |
| **15-25 hours** | Substantial - equivalent to major EC | Primary caregiver role, significant impact on other activities |
| **25+ hours** | Exceptional - near-full-time responsibility | Full caregiver role, would reasonably prevent most other activities |

### Real Student Examples (Valued by Admissions)

- "20 hours during school week, 55 hours/week in summer watching siblings"
- "Daily caregiving for younger siblings morning, after school, and evening"
- "Nighttime support for sibling with panic attacks - woken every night"
- "Farm labor 365 days annually"
- "Working at family restaurant: dishwashing, service, closing"

> "If a student is spending 20 hours a week taking care of a younger sibling, it would be very difficult for that person to also participate in typical high school extracurricular activities."
> — Trisha Ross Anderson, Harvard Making Caring Common

### Types of Family Responsibilities

1. **Sibling Care:** Taking care of younger siblings 4+ hours/week
2. **Elder Care:** Caring for sick, disabled, or elderly family members
3. **Financial Support:** Working to support family income 4+ hours/week
4. **Administrative Support:** Assisting with doctors' appointments, bank visits, visa interviews
5. **Household Management:** Cooking, cleaning, transportation for family
6. **Translation/Interpretation:** Cultural bridge for immigrant families

### Harvard Rating System Integration

- **Rating 5:** "Substantial activity outside of conventional EC participation such as **family commitments or term-time work**"
- **Rating 6:** "Special circumstances limit or prevent participation"

Family/work gets a DEDICATED rating tier at Harvard - not lumped with "weak activities."

### 315 Admission Deans Statement

> "We view substantial family contributions as **very important**, and we encourage students to report them in their applications. **It will only positively impact** the review of their application."
> — June 2020, 300+ institutions

### When to Include vs Exclude

| Include | Exclude |
|---------|---------|
| 4+ hours/week consistent commitment | Typical household chores expected of all teens |
| Impacts ability to do other activities | Occasional help with siblings |
| Required responsibility (not optional) | Voluntary assistance when convenient |
| Ongoing over extended period | One-time or occasional situations |
| Can describe specific impact and skills | Cannot articulate concrete responsibilities |

## Under-Resourced Schools: Calibration Framework

### Rice University's School Classification System (Since 1992)

> "Students are compared with other students from **similar high schools** and not against students who attended more affluent schools."

**Key Principle:** Peer comparison within similar resource bands, not across all applicants.

### College Board Example

> "A student who took one AP course at his or her elite, urban high school with dozens of AP options might well be considered **differently** than a student who took the only AP class available at his or her rural or under-resourced school"

### Pomona College's Differential Expectations

> "We have different expectations for different students: the exam scores from a daughter of two college professors are viewed in a different context than the scores from a first-generation college student who attends an underfunded high school"

### Princeton OCR Compliance

> "University admissions staff reviewed applicants in the context of their secondary school in order to compare their accomplishments given the resources available"

### Implementation Principle

**"Maxing out" available opportunities matters more than absolute achievement level.**

A student who takes every AP available (even if only 2-3) demonstrates more than a student who takes 8 of 20 available.

## Geographic Limitations: Sparse Country

### Severe Underrepresentation Data

| Institution | Rural % | US Rural % | Gap |
|-------------|---------|------------|-----|
| Princeton Class of 2028 | 9% | 19% | -10% |
| Caltech Class of 2027 | 7% | 19% | -12% |
| Vanderbilt Class of 2029 | 10% | 19% | -9% |
| Princeton (Midwest) | 10% | 20% | -10% |

### National Context

- Only 29% of rural residents aged 18-24 enrolled in higher education
- vs. 48% from urban areas and 42% from suburban areas
- Rural poverty rate: 15.4% (vs. 11.9% urban)
- Median rural household income: $11,000 below national average

### Harvard "Sparse Country" Term

Dean Fitzsimmons uses this term for rural areas with intensified recruitment.

> "A student with competitive test scores and a high GPA will undoubtedly receive a second look" from rural areas because they "bring diverse political, academic, and professional interests to classrooms where such perspectives are rare"

### Geographic Advantage Quantification

**Post-SFFA Reality:**
> "In the wake of the 2023 U.S. Supreme Court ruling against the use of race in college admissions, geography plays a greater role in applicant diversity."

**States with Geographic Advantage:**
- Great Plains states (Kansas, Nebraska, Montana, Wyoming, Dakotas)
- Deep South (rural areas)
- Rocky Mountain states (Idaho, Utah)
- Any non-major-metro area

**How Much Advantage?**
- Equal applications from Vermont vs. California → Vermont often preferred for diversity
- But: "If the California applicant was a stronger candidate, then he/she/they would be picked"
- Wealth still trumps geography overall (Raj Chetty research)

### STARS College Network (2023)

- 32 institutions committed to rural recruitment
- $150+ million committed over 10 years
- 11,000+ offers to Class of 2028 (12.9% increase over prior year)
- Includes fly-in programs for rural students

### Resource Constraints AOs Account For

| Constraint | Context Given |
|------------|---------------|
| No AP courses | Only 50% of rural districts offer AP vs 85% suburban |
| Limited extracurricular options | Fewer clubs, sports, activities available |
| No local college counseling | Higher counselor-to-student ratios |
| Work necessity | Higher family financial need |
| Geographic isolation | Limited access to competitions, programs, mentors |

## The Equivalence Principle

### College Board's Critical Comparison

> "A U.S.-born student who did not work during high school and participated in international service ventures during summers, **funded by parents**, may be acknowledged for commitment to others... However, that student might be seen **differently** than a U.S.-born student who had to **work after school due to family responsibilities** and couldn't travel, but was able to demonstrate an **even greater dedication** to help others in need... **Differences may be weighed as equivalent in accomplishment (or not) depending on the context.**"

### Three Revealed Evaluation Principles

1. **Impact over optics:** Depth/authenticity > prestige/visibility
2. **Resource awareness:** Parent-funded activities NOT automatically valued higher
3. **Maturity and sacrifice:** Work/caregiving + academics can OUTWEIGH traditional leadership

### Pay-to-Play Skepticism

> "Admissions officers are **turned off** when experiences repeatedly appear to be **bought—versus earned or self-directed**"
> — Former Ivy League AO via ProPublica

> "If your family depends on you for an extra source of income, you will **not be penalized** for spending your time at a job rather than preparing to win your next international competition"

## Documentation Channels

### Three Primary Channels for Circumstances

| Channel | Best For | Guidance |
|---------|----------|----------|
| **Activities Section** | Work, ongoing family responsibilities | Use "Family Responsibilities" dropdown; include hours/week |
| **Additional Information** | Explaining impact on participation | 650 words; "short, neutral, brief" (Harberson) |
| **Counselor Letter** | Third-party validation | Especially valuable when counselor knows circumstances |

### 2025 Common App "Challenges and Circumstances" Prompt

Explicitly asks about:
- "circumstances that have made it difficult for you to get more involved in extracurricular activities, such as working to support your family"
- "family or other obligations (care-taking, financial support, etc.)"

### Smith College Guidance

> "We encourage students to use the Activities area to list paid jobs and any caretaking of younger siblings or older relatives, or tasks relating to household management, etc."

### How to Present (Best Practices)

1. Use "Family Responsibilities" dropdown in Common App activities
2. Specify hours/week and weeks/year
3. Describe specific tasks and your role
4. Note any impact on other activities (briefly, factually)
5. Consider addressing in Additional Information if extensive

## Information Asymmetry Solutions

### The Core Problem

First-generation and low-income students often don't know what's valued in college admissions.

> "Students are not always able to participate in sports, student government, or other activities because their responsibilities at home preclude doing so. However, students often don't realize that taking care of siblings, working part-time, and similar commitments can demonstrate a high level of leadership, service, and a sense of responsibility."
> — Patricia Peek, Dean of Undergraduate Admission, Fordham University

### What Students Often Don't Know

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| "Work isn't an extracurricular" | Work is HIGHLY valued, especially 10+ hrs/week |
| "Family care doesn't count" | "Cornerstone of college admissions" - can outweigh test scores |
| "Need impressive activities" | Local, sustained impact = international voluntourism |
| "Need many activities" | 2-4 deep activities > 10 surface activities |
| "I have nothing to list" | Jobs, chores, sibling care, translation ALL count |
| "My school has limited clubs" | Context is evaluated - you're not penalized |

### How Admissions Views First-Gen/Low-Income

> "Being a first-generation college student is considered a 'hook' that may cause admissions officers to pay extra attention to an application... Colleges may be more willing to forgive slightly lower grades, test scores, or extracurricular involvement."
> — CollegeVine

### System Implementation Requirements

Our system MUST proactively inform students:

1. **Explicitly tell students:**
   - "Work experience is valued - list your job"
   - "Family responsibilities count - list caregiving, household management, translation"
   - "Local impact is valued - don't undersell community work"
   - "Fewer deep activities beats many shallow ones"

2. **Prompt students who may not realize:**
   - "Do you have a job? This belongs on your activity list"
   - "Do you care for siblings or family members? This is highly valued"
   - "Do you help with a family business? Include this"
   - "Do you translate for your family? This demonstrates valuable skills"

3. **Provide context normalization:**
   - Adjust expectations based on school resources
   - Account for work/family constraints
   - Value depth in limited activities over breadth with privilege

### Resources to Share with Students

- QuestBridge (connects low-income students with full scholarships)
- First-gen mentorship programs
- Making Caring Common guidance
- College Essay Guy first-gen resources
