I'm building a college advising system and need to understand what the top 30 US universities (Ivy League, Stanford, MIT, top privates, top publics) have IN COMMON when evaluating applicants. This is Part 2, focusing on extracurriculars, essays, recommendations, character evaluation, red flags, and the holistic review process.

**Focus on what elite admissions officers consistently value across institutions. Cite specific sources: admissions officers, published guides, research studies, institutional documentation. Use high quality, credible, and reliable sources only.**

## 2. Extracurricular Evaluation: Universal Principles

How do admissions officers at elite schools universally evaluate activities?

### Quality vs. Quantity
- Is there an ideal number of activities? What research or admissions officer statements exist on this?
- What's considered "too few" activities? What's considered "too many" or "padding"?
- How do officers distinguish genuine involvement from resume-building?
- What specific signals indicate depth vs. superficial participation?
- How many hours per week signals "serious commitment" vs. "casual participation"?
- How is consistency (years of involvement) weighted vs. intensity (hours per week)?

### Leadership Evaluation
- What types of leadership actually impress admissions officers (vs. just holding a title)?
- How do officers evaluate "founded an organization" vs. "led an existing organization"?
- What evidence of leadership impact do they look for beyond the title itself?
- When does individual excellence matter more than leadership roles?
- How do they view leadership in small organizations vs. large organizations?
- What do officers say about "president of 5 clubs" vs. "deeply committed to 2"?

### The "Spike" Concept
- How universally do elite schools value a focused "spike" vs. well-rounded profile?
- What makes a spike compelling vs. just narrow or one-dimensional?
- Can applicants have multiple spikes, or does that defeat the purpose?
- How do admissions officers describe the ideal balance of depth and breadth?
- What specific quotes exist from admissions officers about spikes vs. well-rounded applicants?
- How has the spike concept evolved in recent admissions cycles?

### Impact and Achievement
- How do officers assess "impact" claims in applications?
- What's the difference in how they view school-level vs. community vs. state vs. national impact?
- How are activity achievements verified or fact-checked?
- What makes impact claims credible vs. inflated?
- How do they evaluate quantitative claims ("raised $50,000", "served 500 students")?

### Time Commitment and Progression
- What hours/week signals serious commitment at elite institutions?
- How is summer activity involvement weighted vs. school year?
- How do officers view activities dropped after 1-2 years?
- What progression within activities do they want to see?
- How is senior year activity involvement weighted vs. earlier years?

### Context and Circumstances
- How do officers evaluate activities for students who must work jobs?
- How are family responsibilities (caregiving, family business) viewed as activities?
- How do they calibrate expectations for students with limited access to opportunities?
- What do they say about paid work vs. unpaid extracurriculars?

## 3. Essays: Universal Quality Standards

What makes an essay work at ANY elite school?

### Voice and Authenticity
- What does "authentic voice" actually mean in admissions evaluation?
- How do readers detect genuine vs. performative essays?
- What specific characteristics define a strong personal voice?
- How do officers describe essays that feel "real" vs. "polished but hollow"?
- What role does vulnerability play in effective essays?
- How do they view essays that have clearly been heavily edited by counselors/parents?

### Content and Insight
- What depth of self-reflection do elite schools expect?
- How do readers evaluate intellectual curiosity in essays?
- What balance of "showing" vs. "telling" works best?
- How specific should anecdotes and examples be?
- What do officers say about essays that are "well-written but don't reveal anything"?
- How do they evaluate growth, change, or learning demonstrated in essays?

### Topic Selection
- Are there universally "risky" topics to avoid? What are they specifically?
- What are the most overused essay topics that require exceptional execution?
- What makes a common topic work vs. fail?
- How do officers view essays about:
  - Sports injuries/victories
  - Volunteer trips abroad
  - Immigrant/cultural identity
  - Mental health struggles
  - COVID-19 pandemic
  - Death of a family member
  - Passion for a subject/activity
- What topics do admissions officers say they want to see MORE of?

### Craft and Execution
- What writing quality signals "this student will succeed in our classes"?
- How is creativity valued vs. clarity and structure?
- What structural approaches work best for personal statements?
- How do officers view humor in essays?
- What's the ideal balance of narrative vs. reflection?
- How important is the opening line/hook?
- What do officers say about essays that "try too hard"?

### Common Mistakes
- What are the most common essay mistakes admissions officers cite?
- What makes an essay forgettable vs. memorable?
- How do officers describe essays that hurt applications vs. help them?
- What "red flags" appear in essays that concern readers?
