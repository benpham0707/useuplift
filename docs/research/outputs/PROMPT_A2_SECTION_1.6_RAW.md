## Executive Summary

Elite college admissions has systematically integrated contextual review into holistic admissions processes. The core principle: achievements are evaluated RELATIVE to available opportunities, not in absolute terms. Students who work paid jobs, provide family caregiving, or come from under-resourced environments are not penalized—and may be advantaged—when these circumstances are properly documented.

The most striking finding: Sarah Harberson (former Wesleyan/Penn AO) states that family responsibilities are "the CORNERSTONE of college admissions" and can outweigh even "a perfect test score or being #1 in your class."

## SECTION 1.6: Context and Circumstances in Extracurricular Evaluation

### 1.6.1 Paid Work vs. Unpaid Extracurriculars: Admissions Officers' Perspectives

**Explicit Valuation of Paid Employment**

Sarah Harberson, former admissions officer at Wesleyan University and University of Pennsylvania, states directly:

> "Admissions officers are like me. They appreciate students who have the initiative and responsibility to work. **The more hours, the better, especially for the summer.**"

She emphasizes that manual labor jobs—"cleaning toilets, dishwashing, farm work, lawn care"—are particularly impressive because they are:

> "physically harder than an office job, and they give students perspective on the regular working adult"

**Common Application Recognition**

The Common Application platform validates paid work by including **"Family Responsibilities"** as a dedicated category in the Activities section dropdown menu. Common App documentation states:

> "For admission officers, understanding these responsibilities is essential to understanding who you are as a person"

Categories encompassed:
- Routinely taking care of household tasks like cooking, cleaning, and running errands
- Working to provide family income

**Institutional Directives**

Washington University in St. Louis explicitly states:

> "Students who work for pay and/or have time-consuming family responsibilities should include this in their activity list"

**Common Data Set Evidence**

- 88% of top 60 US colleges rate extracurricular activities as "important" or "very important"
- UT Austin: Lists "work experience" as "Important" in admissions decisions
- UCLA: Marks work experience as "Considered"

Work experience receives formal weight in institutional rubrics.

### 1.6.2 Family Responsibilities: Caregiving and Family Business Contributions

**The Cornerstone Principle**

Sarah Harberson articulates why family responsibilities matter fundamentally:

> "Family responsibilities are the **cornerstone of college admissions** because they reveal loyalty, sacrifice, and the love of family a student embodies. **Nothing is more important than that, not even a perfect test score or being #1 in your class.**"

She describes specific examples:
- "taking care of two much younger siblings in the morning, after school, and into the evening hours EVERY DAY because the parents worked two jobs"
- "being woken by their sibling who struggled with panic attacks every night, holding their sibling, talking to them, and reassuring them until the sun rose"

**315 Deans' Collective Statement (June 2020)**

> "The deans recognize that many students have family responsibilities, including supervising younger siblings, caring for sick relatives, or working to provide family income... **We view substantial family contributions as very important**, and we encourage students to report them in their applications. **It will only positively impact the review of their application.**"

**Time Quantification Matters**

> "It is helpful to know, for example, how much time students spent per week taking on a family responsibility, such as taking care of a sick relative"

**Harvard Rating System Integration**

Rating 5: "Substantial activity outside of conventional EC participation such as **family commitments or term-time work** (could be included with other e/c to boost the rating or left as a '5' if it is more representative of the student's commitment)"

Rating 6: "Special circumstances limit or prevent participation (e.g. a physical condition)"

**Lafayette College Equity Policy**

Lafayette limits extracurricular review to the first six activities listed (vs. ten available). President Nicole Hurd explains:

> "first-generation and low-income students often have challenges amassing a high number of activities, and are **unaware that valuable experiences like caregiving or working can be listed**"

### 1.6.3 Under-Resourced Schools and Communities: Calibrating Expectations

**Rice University's Contextual Review Framework**

> "Reading applications within a student's context also allows us to be more fair to students who come from less resourced backgrounds. It is important to remember that not every school is the same, and some may have more access to accelerated AP and IB courses. Those that live in rural areas may have circumstances that hinder them from participating in advanced extracurricular activities. **Contextual review helps us understand what a student has access to and to better evaluate how they are standing out within their community.**"

**Rice's School Classification System (Since 1992)**

> "the greatest challenge in evaluating applicants is that students apply to Rice from high schools whose quality and resources vary significantly. To overcome this challenge and ensure that students are not disadvantaged in the selection process for having attended under-resourced schools, our admissions office implemented a system in 1992 to **classify applicants' high schools according to the resources, curriculum, and college preparation programs offered. Students are compared with other students from similar high schools and not against students who attended more affluent schools.**"

**College Board Example**

> "A student who took one AP course at his or her elite, urban high school with dozens of AP options might well be considered differently than a student who took the only AP class available at his or her rural or under-resourced school or produced an exceptional project on a complex issue in a school with no AP courses"

**Princeton OCR Compliance**

> "University admissions staff reviewed applicants in the context of their secondary school in order to compare their accomplishments given the resources available to those of applicants from similar settings"

**Pomona College's Differential Expectations**

> "We have different expectations for different students: the exam scores from a daughter of two college professors are viewed in a different context than the scores from a first-generation college student who attends an underfunded high school"

**Jerome A. Lucido's Principle**

> "Given unequal educational opportunity, it is incumbent upon admissions [officers] to strive to understand the conditions under which each applicant has performed and to make judgments based on the context of those conditions"

### 1.6.4 Geographic Limitations: Rural and Remote Student Evaluation

**"Sparse Country" Recruitment**

Harvard Dean Fitzsimmons uses the term "sparse country" to describe rural areas where Harvard intensifies recruitment efforts.

> "a student with competitive test scores and a high GPA will undoubtedly receive a second look" if from rural areas, as these students "bring diverse political, academic, and professional interests to classrooms where such perspectives are rare"

**Severe Underrepresentation Data**

| Institution | Rural Representation | US Rural Population |
|-------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Princeton Class of 2028 | 9% | 19% |
| Princeton (Midwest) | 10% | 20% |
| Caltech Class of 2027 | 7% | 19% |
| Vanderbilt Class of 2029 | 10% | 19% |

Princeton admitted at least 20 students from Manhattan alone—many rural ZIP codes sent zero.

**STARS College Network**

Elite universities joined this network providing "specialized support for students and families from rural and small towns."

Northwestern's Paul Compton:
> "The perspectives and voices of students from rural and small towns are invaluable to our exchange of ideas... insights linked to growing up in geographically remote settings, or in communities where college-going isn't necessarily a given after high school"

**UChicago Emerging Rural Leaders Program**

- Fully funded summer programs for rural freshmen and juniors
- Guaranteed scholarships for select enrollees
- Paid summer internships

### 1.6.5 Documentation Channels for Circumstances

**Channel 1: Activities Section**
- "Family Responsibilities" = dedicated dropdown category
- Include hours/week and description
- Washington U directive: "students who work for pay and/or have time-consuming family responsibilities should include this in their activity list"

**Channel 2: Additional Information Section (650 words)**
- 2025 Common App added "Challenges and Circumstances" prompt
- Explicitly asks about circumstances limiting extracurricular involvement
- Sarah Harberson: "a short, neutral, brief statement goes a long way"

**Channel 3: Counselor Letter of Recommendation**
- Provides third-party validation
- Can contextualize student's situation
- Reddit/AO guidance: "discussing your family obligations with your school counselor" is recommended

**Smith College's Specific Guidance**
> "We encourage students to use the Activities area to list paid jobs and any caretaking of younger siblings or older relatives, or tasks relating to household management, etc. Then, if they'd like to emphasize that significant household responsibilities have prevented their involvement in other activities... in the Additional Information section, they can"

### 1.6.6 Equivalence Principle: Paid Work vs. Unpaid Activities

**College Board's Critical Comparison Example**

> "A U.S.-born student who did not work during high school and participated in international service ventures during summers, funded by parents, may be acknowledged for commitment to others, as well as travel, and possibly even multicultural interests. However, that student might be seen differently than a U.S.-born student who had to work after school due to family responsibilities and couldn't travel, but was able to demonstrate an even greater dedication to help others in need and a multicultural commitment through strong, sustained, and mature actions to guide younger siblings and help immigrant families in their church community. **Differences may be weighed as equivalent in accomplishment (or not) depending on the context.**"

**Three Revealed Principles:**
1. **Impact over optics**: Depth/authenticity > prestige/visibility
2. **Resource awareness**: Parent-funded activities not automatically valued higher
3. **Maturity and sacrifice**: Work/caregiving + academics can outweigh traditional leadership titles

**Pay-to-Play Skepticism (Former Ivy AO via ProPublica)**

> "Admissions officers are turned off when experiences repeatedly appear to be **bought—versus earned or self-directed**—in a student's application"

> "if your family depends on you for an extra source of income, you will **not be penalized** for spending your time at a job rather than preparing to win your next international competition"

### 1.6.7 Limitations and Ongoing Challenges

**1. Information Asymmetry**
- Under-resourced students may not know family responsibilities are valued
- Lafayette College's response: Limit review to 6 activities

**2. Counselor Capacity**
- Under-resourced schools: Often 500:1 counselor-to-student ratios
- Limits documentation of individual circumstances

**3. Verification Concerns**
- Family responsibilities lack standardized documentation
- AOs generally trust self-reporting
- May follow up if claims seem inconsistent

**4. Yield Protection Risk (Rachel Rubin's Harvard research)**
- Some institutions do initial cuts based on "institutional fit" or ability to pay
- Can disadvantage working-class students despite contextual review policies

**5. Rural Recruitment Gaps**
- AOs visit suburbs more than rural areas
- Creates awareness gaps
