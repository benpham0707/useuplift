#### Celebration

Your phrase 'Built data pipeline processing 50,000 patient records' instantly separates you from 95% of high school research assistants -- most say 'helped with data analysis.' You BUILT something, and you quantified the scale.

- Technical ownership language ('Built') signals independent contribution rather than observation
- Specific scale metric (50,000 records) demonstrates real-world research complexity, not toy datasets
- Co-authorship claim provides external validation of intellectual contribution

> REF: "Built data pipeline processing 50,000 patient records" [strength] ownership + scale (MATCH)
> REF: "Co-authored paper" [strength] validation signal (MATCH)

#### Tier Assessment: 3

This activity sits at Tier 3 because it demonstrates genuine research participation with technical contribution (the pipeline), but lacks the external validation or independent intellectual contribution that defines Tier 2 research. The paper is 'submitted' (outcome pending) to an undergraduate journal (lower validation bar than peer-reviewed venues). Under Level 3 constraints, this evaluates as Tier 2-equivalent -- building technical infrastructure while working 25 hrs/week shows exceptional initiative.

**What makes this tier:** The 'Built data pipeline' phrase proves you contributed technical infrastructure, not just data entry. The 50,000 records scale shows real research complexity. However, 'Worked with professor' frames you as assistant rather than co-investigator, and 'submitted to undergraduate journal' signals pending validation. The gap between Tier 3 and Tier 2 is: did YOU design the research question, or execute someone else's design?

#### Strengths

**Technical ownership language combined with scale metric**

*Why:* When an AO reads 'Built data pipeline processing 50,000 patient records,' they immediately categorize you as a builder, not an observer. Most research descriptions say 'assisted with data analysis' or 'helped process patient records' -- passive language that signals you watched someone else work. 'Built' is an active verb that claims authorship. The 50,000 number prevents the AO from imagining a toy project -- this is real research infrastructure handling real-world data complexity. Together, these two elements answer the AO's core question: 'What did THIS student actually DO?'

*Leverage:* Use this strength to anchor your 'Additional Information' section: 'My research pipeline automated analysis that previously required 40+ hours of manual work, enabling the team to expand from 1 county to 15 counties.' This shows you understand the IMPACT of your technical contribution. In interviews, lead with the problem: 'Rural healthcare data was scattered across incompatible systems. I built a pipeline to unify it.' Problem -> Solution -> Scale is the research narrative that resonates.

**Co-authorship as validation signal**

*Why:* Co-authorship on a submitted paper is the difference between 'I did research' and 'A professor trusted me enough to attach their name to my work.' In academia, authorship is currency -- professors don't add student names unless the contribution merits it. This signals to AOs that your work met a professional standard, not just a high school standard. Even if the paper isn't accepted yet, the fact that a professor deemed your contribution publication-worthy is meaningful validation.

*Leverage:* If the paper gets accepted before applications are due, immediately update your activities list and notify admissions offices. If it's still under review, use your Additional Information section: 'Paper currently under review at [Journal Name]; I contributed the data processing pipeline and co-wrote the methodology section.' This specificity shows you understand academic authorship norms. In interviews, be ready to explain YOUR specific contribution: 'I'm second author because I built the technical infrastructure and contributed to the analysis, while Dr. [Name] designed the research question and led the writing.'

#### Improvements

**Issue: Opening with 'Worked with professor' frames you as assistant, not researcher** [HIGH]

*Why:* The first three words of your description create the AO's mental category for you. 'Worked with professor' triggers the 'research assistant' frame -- someone who helps with someone else's project. This is the most common research description AOs see, and it signals Tier 3-4. Compare to 'Built NLP pipeline analyzing...' which triggers the 'researcher/builder' frame -- someone who created something. In the 8-minute read, the AO won't re-read your description to revise their first impression. You get ONE categorization. Make it count.

*Fix:* Lead with YOUR contribution, not the collaboration structure. The professor relationship is context, not the headline. Structure: [What you built/did] + [Problem it solved] + [Scale/validation]. The professor can appear later as validation: 'Built NLP pipeline analyzing rural healthcare access (50K patient records); co-authored paper with Dr. [Name] submitted to [Journal].' This structure puts YOUR work first, then adds the professor as credibility boost.

```
Before: "Worked with professor on NLP project analyzing rural healthcare access patterns."
After:  "Built NLP pipeline analyzing rural healthcare access (50K patient records); co-authored paper with Dr. [Name] submitted to [Journal]."
```

**Issue: Missing the 'so what' -- what changed because of your work?** [HIGH]

*Why:* You built a pipeline. You processed 50,000 records. You co-authored a paper. An AO reading this thinks: 'Okay, but what HAPPENED because of this research?' Did the findings influence policy? Did your pipeline enable new research? Did the analysis reveal something surprising? Without the outcome, this reads like a technical exercise, not research with purpose. The committee pitch test: if your advocate says 'This student built a data pipeline,' the room shrugs. If they say 'This student's pipeline revealed a 40% healthcare access gap that led to new telehealth funding,' the room remembers you.

*Fix:* Add ONE sentence about impact: what the research revealed, what it enabled, or what happened next. If the paper findings aren't final, describe what the pipeline ENABLED: 'Pipeline enabled analysis of 15 counties (previously limited to 1)' or 'Findings revealed 40% urban-rural access disparity.' If you don't know the impact yet, describe the research GOAL: 'to identify barriers to telehealth adoption in underserved areas.' Even stating the purpose gives the AO a reason to care.

```
Before: "Built data pipeline processing 50,000 patient records. Co-authored paper submitted to undergraduate journal."
After:  "Built data pipeline processing 50,000 patient records; analysis revealed 40% urban-rural telehealth access gap. Co-authored paper submitted to [Journal]."
```

**Issue: 'Submitted to undergraduate journal' signals pending validation, not achievement** [HIGH]

*Why:* In research, 'submitted' means 'we hope this is good enough.' 'Accepted' or 'published' means 'external experts confirmed this is good enough.' AOs know the difference. Undergraduate journals vary wildly in selectivity -- some accept 80%+ of submissions. Without acceptance, the AO can't gauge quality. This is especially critical for Technical Innovation Schools, where research rigor matters. If the paper is accepted before you apply, this becomes a Tier 2 signal. If not, you need other validation (conference presentation, professor letter emphasizing your contribution).

*Fix:* If accepted before applications: change to 'Published in [Journal Name]' or 'Accepted to [Journal Name] (15% acceptance rate)' if you can find the rate. If still under review: add alternative validation: 'Co-authored paper (under review); presented findings at State Undergraduate Research Symposium' or 'Co-authored paper (under review); pipeline now used by 3 other research teams.' This shows the work has value even without publication. If rejected: pivot to the technical contribution: 'Built NLP pipeline (50K records) enabling multi-county healthcare analysis; findings presented at [venue].'

```
Before: "Co-authored paper submitted to undergraduate journal."
After:  "Co-authored paper accepted to Journal of Undergraduate Research in Health Informatics; presented findings at State Research Symposium."
```

*Fix:* Document three numbers: (1) How many employees do you typically supervise per shift? (2) What's the approximate transaction volume or revenue you're responsible for? (3) What specific decisions are you authorized to make without manager approval?

```
Before: "Promoted to shift lead after 6 months"
After:  "Promoted to shift lead after 6 months; manage 8-person team during peak hours; oversee $18K+ daily transactions"
```

**Issue: Hidden impact -- no evidence of what changed because of your leadership** [HIGH]

*Why:* You've held this shift lead role for potentially 6+ months. In that time, you've made SOME impact -- reduced wait times, improved inventory accuracy, solved a recurring problem, created a training system. But none of that appears in your description.
