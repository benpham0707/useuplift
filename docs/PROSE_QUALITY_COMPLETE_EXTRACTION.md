## Executive Summary

This research covers sentence-level writing craft and authentic voice - two critical dimensions that distinguish memorable essays from forgettable ones. The key finding: **authenticity trumps polish**. Admissions officers consistently prefer "rough edges that feel authentic" over "perfectly edited but lifeless" prose.

### Key Themes

1. **Voice = Personality in Writing** - Natural vocabulary, thought patterns, personality markers
2. **Sentence Craft = Music** - Length variation creates rhythm and engagement
3. **Verbs > Adjectives** - Strong verbs beat adjective-heavy writing every time
4. **Specificity Always Wins** - Concrete nouns outperform generic + adjectives
5. **Over-Editing Kills Voice** - Excessive polish raises authenticity red flags
6. **Read Aloud Test** - The single best revision technique for authenticity

## Section 1: What Is "Voice" in Writing

### 1.1 Admissions Officer Definitions

| Source | Quote | Application |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| Dr. Tina Brooks (Pomona) | "Admissions officers aren't seeking polished academic prose—they want to hear how students think" | Don't over-polish |
| Tom (Pomona Admissions) | "Voice can help them see how you fit in the village they want to create" | Voice reveals fit |
| Former Ivy League AO | "We'd rather see an essay with rough edges that feels authentic than one that's perfectly edited but lifeless. If it sounds like it was written by a 40-year-old, we know something's off" | Age-appropriate is critical |
| College Confidential | Essays should flow "like a delightful conversation, free of fluffy words and phrases" | Conversational naturalness |

### 1.2 Voice Components

**Natural Vocabulary**
- Words students would "actually employ in thoughtful conversation"
- NOT "fancy thesaurus synonyms you'd personally never use"
- AOs "can really tell the difference between an overwritten, overcoached essay, and your natural voice"

**Thought Patterns**
- How students "process experiences and connect ideas"
- "The monologue in their head becomes audible through word choices"

**Personality Markers**
- "Distinctive style, sense of humor and natural phrasing"
- Self-deprecating humor vs. earnest enthusiasm - neither superior, must be genuine

### 1.3 How AOs Detect Manufactured Voice

| Detection Method | What It Reveals |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Consistency across application | Essays vs. rec letters vs. interviews should align |
| Age-appropriate expression | "Words like 'insouciantly' would raise a giant red flag" |
| Two-space test | Two spaces between sentences signals adult involvement |
| Conversational flow | Stiff formality questions authenticity |
| AI detection patterns | Generic phrasing, lack of specific details, formulaic structure |

## Section 2: Sentence-Level Craft

### 2.1 Gary Provost's Famous Demonstration

```
"This sentence has five words. Here are five more words. Five-word sentences are fine.
But several together become monotonous. Listen to what is happening. The writing is getting
boring. The sound of it drones. It's like a stuck record. The ear demands some variety.

Now listen. I vary the sentence length, and I create music. Music. The writing sings.
It has a pleasant rhythm, a lilt, a harmony. I use short sentences. And I use sentences
of medium length. And sometimes, when I am certain the reader is rested, I will engage
him with a sentence of considerable length, a sentence that burns with energy and builds
with all the impetus of a crescendo, the roll of the drums, the crash of the cymbals–
sounds that say listen to this, it is important."
```

**Key Insight**: Monotonous = same length sentences. Musical = varied lengths.

### 2.2 Sentence Length Guidelines

| Principle | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Short sentences = punch | "Great way to emphasize important points" |
| Long sentences = rhythm | "Add rhythm to your writing" |
| Uniform brevity = children's book | "Dick and Jane" feel |
| Uniform length = droning | "The ear demands variety" |
| Strategic variation = music | Let content dictate length |

### 2.3 Verb Strength (Critical for Detection)

**Weak Verbs** (RED FLAGS):
- State-of-being: is, am, are, was, were, be, being, been
- Generic + adverb: "walked slowly," "ate quickly"
- Vague: had, made, got, went

**Strong Verbs** (GREEN FLAGS):
- Specific action: tiptoed, devoured, sprinted, whispered
- Sensory: pounded, shimmered, crackled
- Precise: negotiated, orchestrated, discovered

**Transformation Examples**:
| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "She walked quietly" | "She tiptoed" |
| "He ate quickly" | "He devoured" |
| "The music was loud" | "The music pounded my eardrums" |
| "I harness salient people skills" | "I engage with customers at my Target register" |

### 2.4 Elements of Style Principle

> "Write with nouns and verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs" - Strunk & White

**Jerry Jenkins**: "Good writing is more about well-chosen nouns and powerful verbs than adjectives and adverbs"

## Section 3: Rhythm and Flow

### 3.1 Rhythm Matches Emotion

| Emotion | Sentence Pattern | Example |
|---------|-----------------|---------|
| Fear/Panic | Short, repetitive, dropped pronouns | "She couldn't get out, couldn't get out, couldn't get out" |
| Peace/Calm | Long, specific details, multisyllabic | "Beneath the willows and oaks, I smelled the thick scent of the swimming pool" |
| Tension | Building length, acceleration | Short → medium → long crescendo |
| Impact | Very short after long setup | "Stop crying. It's just a belt buckle." |

### 3.2 Short Sentences for Impact

**The Mary Karr Technique** (from *The Liars' Club*):
- Build scene with longer sentences
- Drop devastating short sentence at end
- Creates pause for emotional processing

**College Essay Applications**:
- Emphasis: "Perfection. It's a tricky little concept."
- Pace control: Speed up action sequences
- Voice marker: "Not happening. I had other plans."

### 3.3 Structural Variation Without Gimmicks

**Vary Openings** (avoid too many starting with "I", "The", "This"):
- Prepositional: "In the laboratory, I discovered..."
- Adverbial: "While studying quantum mechanics, I realized..."
- Participial: "Exhausted from the competition, I..."

**Match Structure to Content**:
- Fast action = short, staccato sentences
- Contemplation = longer, flowing sentences
- Emphasis = strategic fragment

### 3.4 Read Aloud Test

**Why It Works**:
- Activates speech, hearing, and memory centers
- "If you stumble over a sentence, your reader likely will too"
- Catches repetition invisible to silent reading
- Reveals if writing "sounds like you"

**Duke Writing Studio**: "Pay close attention to sentences or passages that make you stumble as you read"

## Section 4: Word Choice Precision

### 4.1 Mark Twain's Dictum

> "The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter—'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning."

### 4.2 Four Levels of Word Choice

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Denotative precision | Accurate meaning | "Mansion" vs "big house" |
| Connotative resonance | Emotional associations | "Utilize" sounds bureaucratic |
| Specificity | Information density | "Max, my golden retriever" vs "dog" |
| Context appropriateness | Audience fit | Technical jargon vs accessible |

### 4.3 Specific Nouns Beat Adjective Stacking

| Adjective-Heavy | Specific Noun |
|-----------------|---------------|
| "A big, red, shiny car" | "Ferrari" |
| "A small, cozy house" | "Cottage" |
| "I ate lunch" | "I devoured a baloney sandwich" |
| "I work at a store" | "I work at Target" |
| "A very large house" | "Mansion" |
| "A small, quick dog" | "Terrier" |

**Academic Research**: "Many adjective–noun combinations can be replaced entirely by a more specific noun"

### 4.4 Simple vs Sophisticated Vocabulary

**Case FOR sophisticated**:
- Shows "comfort with the English language"
- Signals readiness for college discourse

**Case FOR simple**:
- "More important that essay sound like applicant"
- Thesaurus alternatives often sound forced

**Resolution**: Use sophisticated vocabulary you **genuinely employ** in thoughtful conversation. Test: Would you say "elucidate" to a teacher? If no, use "explain."

### 4.5 Essay-Speak vs Natural Language (CRITICAL)

**Essay-Speak Red Flags**:
- Forced formality (eliminating contractions)
- Third-person constructions
- Thesaurus syndrome ("showcase" → "exhibit")
- Passive voice to sound academic
- "Fluffy words and phrases"

**Natural Language Green Flags**:
- Contractions ("they're," "don't")
- First-person throughout
- Words you'd actually use
- Active voice
- Conversational rhythm

**Student Quote**: "My counselor tells me I shouldn't sound like I'm having a conversation with my friend when I'm writing. But isn't that the point? The admissions officer should read my essay and feel like they know who I am."

## Section 5: Imagery and Metaphor

### 5.1 When Metaphors STRENGTHEN Essays

| Criterion | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Emerges organically | "Don't try to fit an existing essay into a metaphor" |
| Provides structure without overwhelming | "Give definition to the entire essay" while keeping narrative central |
| Creates fresh connections | Juxtaposes unlike elements for new insight |
| Uses accessible comparison objects | From student's actual experience (e.g., dented truck) |

**Memorable Example** (Greg Zaiser): "The student who used the dents on his truck as a metaphor for his life challenges. It personalized the experience."

### 5.2 When Metaphors WEAKEN Essays

| Red Flag | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| Overused comparisons | "Journey," "mountain climbing," "puzzle" - exhausted |
| Obscures rather than clarifies | If needs explanation, it fails |
| Signals tryhard | "You will almost surely come off as trying too hard" |
| Prioritizes cleverness | Calls attention to ingenuity, distracts from content |

### 5.3 Concrete Imagery for Abstract Topics

**Research Finding**: "Almost a one-to-one correlation between how concrete a passage was and how easy it was to understand"

**Why It Works**:
- Visual processing engages different neural networks
- Specific details create believability
- Concrete nouns reduce need for adjectives

**Transformation**:
| Abstract | Concrete |
|----------|----------|
| "I learned about different cultures" | "The kissing each cheek to greet. Steamy empanada pastries. Late dinners with heated arguments about River vs Boca soccer teams" |
| "I am hardworking" | "I rise at 5am, seven days a week, and run two miles, no matter the weather" |

### 5.4 Forced Imagery Warning Signs

| Warning Sign | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| Generic descriptors | "Beautiful sunset" vs "sky fractured into orange and purple" |
| Emotion labels | "I was nervous" vs "My heart raced in my chest" |
| Predictable progression | "Big game," mission trip, overcoming obstacles |
| Melodramatic overstatement | "Scared to death," "cried my eyes out," "worst nightmare" |
| Purple prose | Excessive ornamentation drawing attention to itself |

**Litmus Test**: "Could this imagery appear in anyone's essay?" If yes, it's too generic.

## Section 6: Maintaining Voice Across Essays

### 6.1 Voice vs Tone

| Voice | Tone |
|-------|------|
| WHO you are | HOW you express yourself in context |
| Remains constant | Shifts appropriately |
| Vocabulary, rhythms, perspective | Formality, emphasis, mood |

### 6.2 Appropriate Tonal Variation

| Prompt Type | Appropriate Shift |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Formal (Why This College) | More structure, strategic sophistication |
| Personal narrative | More emotional openness, reflection |
| Creative/quirky | More personality, humor |
| Activity descriptions | More accomplishment focus |

**But**: Underlying voice - vocabulary preferences, sentence rhythms, perspective - should remain recognizable.

### 6.3 The Consistency Test

**Why It Matters**:
- "Inconsistencies can raise red flags"
- AOs read holistically, form mental models
- Voice shifts suggest different authors

**How to Test**:
1. Read all essays aloud consecutively
2. Ask friends/family: "Does this sound like me?"
3. Check vocabulary level consistency
4. Analyze sentence patterns
5. Look for same personality markers

**Legitimate Variation** (OK):
- Emphasis shifts (vulnerability vs leadership)
- Formality gradation
- Content-driven rhythm changes

**Illegitimate Variation** (RED FLAG):
- Dramatic vocabulary shifts
- Completely different sentence patterns
- Personality marker disappearance

## Section 7: Editing for Voice

### 7.1 How Over-Editing Kills Voice

**Mechanisms**:

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Vocabulary substitution | Uncle's edits add "insouciantly" - raises red flag |
| Structural homogenization | Imposing conventional structure kills organic organization |
| Personality smoothing | Eliminating quirks, fragments, informal constructions |
| Emotional dampening | Sanitizing raw expression for "safety" |

**Quote**: "Some essays are so over-edited and polished, the student's voice has been squeezed out"

### 7.2 Signs of "Polished to Death"

**Linguistic Tells**:
- Vocabulary inconsistency with rest of application
- Unnaturally polished construction (no sentence variation)
- Complete absence of contractions/fragments
- Generic eloquence without specific details

**Content Signals**:
- Emotional distance (raw admissions removed)
- Thematic obviousness (everything over-explained)
- Loss of experimentation (creativity conventionalized)

### 7.3 Parent/Counselor Editing Problems

**Why They Over-Edit**:
- Anxiety about outcomes
- Generational writing conventions (formal = better)
- Misunderstanding of priorities (polish vs authenticity)

**Consequences**:
- Voice inconsistency across essays
- Detection by AOs ("sounds like a 40-year-old")
- Psychological impact on student confidence

**Appropriate Involvement**:
- "Vibe check" - noting if too braggy/self-loathing
- "Connecting the dots" - where readers need context
- "So what?" - encouraging deeper reflection
- Grammatical errors
- Asking clarifying questions

**Inappropriate Involvement**:
- Rewriting sentences
- Substituting vocabulary
- Changing tone/personality markers
- Suggesting topics (instead of helping discover)
- "Punch up vocabulary" - DANGER ZONE

### 7.4 Improving Without Losing Authenticity

**Process-Based**:
- Separate content from polish phases
- Multiple drafts with different purposes
- Read aloud throughout

**Craft-Focused Improvements** (voice-preserving):
| Improvement | How to Preserve Voice |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Strengthen verbs | Within student's vocabulary range |
| Add specificity | Details, not sophistication |
| Vary sentence length | Same word choices, different rhythm |
| Trim redundancy | Eliminate repetition, keep voice |

**Feedback Protocol**:
- Ask reviewers to identify clarity issues, authenticity concerns, grammar errors
- Explicitly request NO sentence rewrites or vocabulary changes
- Multiple shallow reviews > one deep edit

## Section 8: Admissions Perspectives

### 8.1 What AOs Notice About Writing Quality

**Primary Dimensions**:
| Dimension | What They Seek |
|-----------|---------------|
| Authenticity | "Writing that unmistakably belongs to its author" |
| Storytelling | "Compelling story" over "slick, polished style" |
| Self-reflection | Ability to analyze experiences, extract meaning |
| Specific details | "Could only come from genuine experience" |

**What They Flag**:
- Clarity issues
- Effort level (too much = suspicious; too little = not invested)
- Opening impact
- Voice consistency

**What They Overlook**:
- Minor grammatical imperfections
- "Rough edges"
- Technical perfection (if inauthentic)

### 8.2 Does Prose Quality Affect Decisions?

**NACAC Data**:
- 18.9% "Considerable Importance"
- 37.3% "Moderate Importance"
- 56.2% combined importance

**How It Influences**:
| Function | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| Tiebreaker | Distinguishes "maybe" applicants |
| Builds advocacy | Memorable details argued in committee |
| Prevents rejection | Bad essays can sink strong applications |

**"Good Enough" Threshold**:
- Most essays neither help nor hurt significantly
- Exceptional essays provide meaningful advantage
- Terrible essays actively damage
- Bar = authentic, coherent expression of genuine perspective

### 8.3 Memorable vs Forgettable Essays

**Most Memorable** (Greg Zaiser):
> "An applicant who described her brother's special needs. Instead of taking a path I expected, she revealed that he embarrassed her and that she found herself trying to keep her friends from meeting him. It was raw, real, completely uncomfortable and incredibly authentic. Risky? Perhaps. But it stood out because she was 'real'."

**What Makes Essays Memorable**:
- Emotional resonance (evokes genuine response)
- Distinctive perspective
- Specific details
- Authentic voice

**What Makes Essays Forgettable**:
- Trying to sound smart instead of themselves
- Writing about subjects they don't care about
- Sanitized narratives
- Generic eloquence
