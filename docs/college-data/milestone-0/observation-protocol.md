# College Discovery Observation Protocol

## Research question

When a student starts with a broad field, place, school-type, setting, or budget
preference, what information helps them decide whether an unfamiliar college is
worth investigating further—and what makes them distrust or abandon the result?

This is behavioral observation, not a usability test of the proposed mockup. Do
not teach students Uplift's planned vocabulary before the session.

## Participant target

Recruit five U.S. high-school students who expect to research colleges within the
next two years. Seek variation in grade, geography, first-generation status,
budget awareness, and prior college-search experience. Do not collect immigration
status, household income, transcripts, test scores, or other sensitive details
unless a later reviewed protocol makes them necessary.

For minors, obtain the consent/assent required by the research setting. Assign
participant IDs `P01` through `P05`; do not put student names in this repository.

## Setup

- 30–40 minutes, remote or in person.
- Student uses their normal browser and any public sources they choose.
- Observer records screen only with consent; otherwise take timestamped notes.
- Do not log account credentials, private messages, precise address, or search
  history unrelated to the task.
- Moderator may clarify the task but must not suggest sites, schools, filters, or
  decision criteria during the unassisted portion.

## Core task

> Imagine you want to find two colleges worth learning more about. At least one
> should be a school you did not already know well. Use the internet as you
> normally would. Think aloud about what you look for, what you trust, and why you
> keep or reject an option. This is not a test of you and there is no right list.

If the student cannot begin after two minutes, record the stall before offering:

> Start with any one thing you care about—for example a field, a place, a type of
> school, a campus setting, or a rough annual budget.

## Observer rules

1. Capture behavior before asking why.
2. Record the exact source and reporting year the student notices; do not infer
   that a visible number was understood.
3. Mark observer prompts separately from spontaneous student actions.
4. Never label a school reach, match, safety, good, bad, or affordable for them.
5. Ask neutral follow-ups: “What made you open that?”, “What does that number mean
   to you?”, “What would you verify next?”, and “What makes this trustworthy?”

## Closing questions

Ask only after the unassisted task:

1. Which information most changed your mind?
2. Which information did you distrust or want to verify?
3. Was a reporting year or source visible? Did it matter?
4. What did “cost” mean to you: tuition, total cost, or likely price after aid?
5. Did any school appear that you would not have found otherwise?
6. What would make you save a school for later?

## Analysis method

After all five sessions, code observations—not personality judgments—under:

- starting constraint;
- keep/reject decision;
- source and freshness behavior;
- affordability interpretation;
- program-evidence interpretation;
- confusion/stall;
- trust/verification;
- unfamiliar-school discovery;
- save/revisit intent.

Count a pattern as repeated when observed in at least three sessions. Keep
important minority and safety findings visible, but label their frequency. Do not
convert qualitative observations into admission probability or ranking weights.
